# New York Homeowners Appetite Guide






## H.0 — Purpose and Status

**H.0.1** This guide states the New York homeowners underwriting appetite and supports consistent risk selection. You should use it to identify submissions that fit the company’s intended market because consistent selection supports sound portfolio management.

**H.0.2** This guide is internal underwriting guidance and is not part of any insurance contract. You must not represent its statements as coverage terms because coverage is determined by the applicable insurance contract.

**H.0.3** You may use this guide when evaluating new business, renewal business, and changes to existing risks. This approach promotes consistent treatment of similar property and occupancy characteristics.

**H.0.4** You should evaluate the complete submission rather than rely on any isolated characteristic. A full review is necessary because individual risk features can affect the significance of other underwriting information.

**H.0.5** You may request clarification or supporting information when the submission does not provide a reliable view of the risk. Additional information is necessary because underwriting decisions must rest on accurate and current facts.

**H.0.6** You should apply reasonable underwriting judgment when a risk presents circumstances not expressly addressed in this guide. Judgment is necessary because unusual facts may create exposure beyond the ordinary appetite indicators.

**H.0.7** You must not use this guide to waive a required underwriting review or approval process. Required review protects the company from accepting risks without appropriate authority.

**H.0.8** You should communicate material underwriting concerns clearly in the file. Clear documentation is necessary because it supports consistent decisions and later review.

**H.0.9** You may decline, restrict, or refer a risk when its characteristics fall outside the company’s appetite. Referral is appropriate because risks outside the appetite may require specialized evaluation.

**H.0.10** You should consider property condition, maintenance, occupancy, and loss history together when assessing eligibility. These factors are related because each may indicate the likelihood or severity of future loss.

**H.0.11** You must evaluate representations made by an applicant for internal consistency. Consistent information is important because conflicting statements may indicate that material risk details require verification.

**H.0.12** You should identify hazards that may affect property damage, liability exposure, or loss adjustment complexity. Early identification is necessary because the underwriting response may depend on the nature of the hazard.

**H.0.13** You may rely on verified information from reliable sources when evaluating a submission. Verification supports sound decisions because unverified information may not accurately reflect current conditions.

**H.0.14** You should refer questions about the ALE percentage when the requested treatment does not align with the underwriting approach. Referral is necessary because this feature can affect the overall risk evaluation.

**H.0.15** You should refer questions concerning the loss assessment additional coverage limit when the submission presents unusual association or ownership circumstances. Referral is appropriate because those circumstances may create exposure not apparent from the property description alone.

**H.0.16** You should identify any issue involving the sworn statement deadline for appropriate handling. Prompt attention is necessary because claim-related obligations may affect the company’s response to a reported loss.

**H.0.17** You should identify whether the sump overflow deductible requires underwriting attention for the submitted risk. This review is necessary because water-related loss characteristics may affect risk acceptability.

**H.0.18** You must not alter coverage, conditions, or claim obligations through an underwriting notation or communication. This restriction is necessary because internal guidance cannot change contractual rights or duties.

**H.0.19** You should record the basis for any exception, referral, or adverse underwriting decision. The record is necessary because it shows that the decision followed a reasoned assessment of the available facts.

**H.0.20** You may seek internal assistance when information is incomplete, unusual, or difficult to evaluate. Consultation is appropriate because specialized review can improve the quality of the underwriting decision.

**H.0.21** You should apply this guide in good faith and without unfair discrimination. Fair application is necessary because underwriting decisions must rest on legitimate risk-related considerations.

**H.0.22** You must treat this guide as operational direction rather than a promise of acceptance, renewal, pricing, or coverage. This distinction is necessary because each submission remains subject to underwriting review and applicable contractual terms.

## H.2 — Roof Age and Condition

**H.2.1** You may bind only when the roof appears serviceable, weather-tight, and free of material deterioration. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because roof condition is a material indicator of expected property loss.

**H.2.2** Obtain clear exterior roof images when the roof condition cannot be confirmed from the application. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because underwriting must support the eligibility decision with observable evidence.

**H.2.3** Refer a risk when roof images show missing, lifted, cracked, curled, or displaced surfacing. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because exposed roof areas may permit water entry.

**H.2.4** Refer a risk when the roof deck appears uneven, bowed, soft, or visibly sagging. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because deck movement may indicate structural weakness or concealed moisture damage.

**H.2.5** Do not rely solely on an applicant statement that the roof is in good condition. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because roof defects may not be apparent to an occupant.

**H.2.6** Review available inspection findings when the application identifies a prior roof concern. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because prior observations may identify unresolved maintenance issues.

**H.2.7** Refer risks with evidence of repeated patching or temporary roof repairs. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because recurring repairs may indicate that the roof system is nearing failure.

**H.2.8** You may accept a professionally repaired roof when the repair appears complete and the surrounding roof remains serviceable. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because a sound repair can restore weather resistance.

**H.2.9** Refer a risk when a repair appears temporary, incomplete, or inconsistent with the surrounding roof material. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because an inadequate repair may not withstand ordinary weather conditions.

**H.2.10** Confirm that roof surfacing is appropriate for the roof slope and construction. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because unsuitable surfacing can increase the likelihood of water intrusion.

**H.2.11** Refer roofs with visible ponding, standing water, or debris accumulation. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because retained moisture can accelerate deterioration of the roof system.

**H.2.12** Refer flat or low-slope roofs when the surfacing condition cannot be adequately evaluated. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because concealed membrane damage can be difficult to identify from limited imagery.

**H.2.13** Verify that roof drainage components appear present and functional where applicable. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because inadequate drainage can contribute to roof and water losses.

**H.2.14** Refer a risk when gutters, leaders, or roof drains appear detached, blocked, or poorly maintained. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because drainage failures may direct water into the building envelope.

**H.2.15** Refer a risk when tree limbs contact, overhang, or visibly abrade the roof surface. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because contact from vegetation can damage surfacing and obstruct drainage.

**H.2.16** Require clarification when rooftop equipment, solar equipment, or similar installations may affect the roof condition. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because penetrations and attachments can create leakage points.

**H.2.17** Confirm that flashing appears intact around chimneys, vents, skylights, and roof transitions. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because flashing failures are a common source of water entry.

**H.2.18** Refer a risk when flashing is rusted, separated, missing, or visibly deteriorated. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because compromised flashing may permit hidden moisture damage.

**H.2.19** Refer a risk when skylights show cracking, staining, failed seals, or damaged surrounding materials. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because skylight openings require reliable integration with the roof system.

**H.2.20** Review interior images when exterior roof observations suggest possible leakage. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because interior staining or deterioration may confirm an active roof issue.

**H.2.21** Refer a risk when ceilings, walls, or attic areas show staining that may be related to roof leakage. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because water damage may continue even when the exterior defect is not obvious.

**H.2.22** Do not treat cosmetic staining as resolved without evidence that the source of moisture has been corrected. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because old staining can conceal continuing leakage.

**H.2.23** Consider attic ventilation and moisture observations when they are available. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because inadequate ventilation can shorten roof surfacing life and contribute to condensation damage.

**H.2.24** Refer a risk when attic observations show moisture, mold-like growth, daylight through the roof deck, or damaged insulation. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because these conditions may indicate roof failure or ventilation concerns.

**H.2.25** Confirm the roof surfacing type when it affects inspection, valuation, or loss assessment. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because different surfacing materials have different maintenance and damage characteristics.

**H.2.26** Refer a risk when the roof surfacing type cannot be identified from reliable evidence. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because accurate classification is necessary to evaluate roof eligibility.

**H.2.27** Evaluate roof surfacing age threshold as an underwriting review concept rather than as a substitute for condition evidence. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because a roof may appear sound or impaired regardless of its reported age.

**H.2.28** Request support for the reported roof age when the application information conflicts with inspection evidence. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because inconsistent information may affect risk selection and claim handling.

**H.2.29** Accept reliable contractor documentation when it clearly identifies completed roof work and the affected roof areas. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because documentation can help distinguish replacement work from limited repair work.

**H.2.30** Refer contractor documentation that is incomplete, unclear, or inconsistent with the observed roof condition. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because unsupported documentation may not establish current roof eligibility.

**H.2.31** Do not assume that a roof has been fully replaced merely because the applicant reports recent work. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because work may be limited to selected areas or components.

**H.2.32** Distinguish roof replacement from roof repair, recoating, overlay work, and maintenance work. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because the scope of work affects the remaining exposure.

**H.2.33** Refer a risk with layered roof surfacing when the condition of the underlying system is uncertain. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because concealed deterioration may remain beneath an overlay.

**H.2.34** Refer a risk when roof edges, eaves, ridges, or valleys show visible deterioration. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because these areas are especially vulnerable to wind and water damage.

**H.2.35** Refer a risk when roof surfacing granules, fragments, or other material loss are visible in gutters or on the ground. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because material loss may indicate reduced weather protection.

**H.2.36** Refer a risk when shingles appear brittle, excessively worn, or unable to lie flat. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because weakened surfacing may be more susceptible to wind and water damage.

**H.2.37** Refer a risk when metal roofing shows corrosion, open seams, loose fasteners, or displaced panels. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because these conditions may compromise the roof’s water-shedding function.

**H.2.39** Refer a risk when wood roof surfacing shows splitting, cupping, rot, moss growth, or material displacement. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because organic surfacing can deteriorate when moisture is retained.

**H.2.40** Consider roof geometry when evaluating the adequacy of submitted imagery. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because complex roofs may conceal condition concerns from ground-level views.

**H.2.41** Request additional imagery when dormers, valleys, roof transitions, or concealed roof faces cannot be evaluated. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because hidden areas may contain the most significant defects.

**H.2.42** Refer a risk when visible roof damage appears related to wind, falling objects, fire, ice, or other external causes. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because damage may require repair before coverage can be offered.

**H.2.43** Do not bind a risk with an active roof leak or unresolved water entry condition. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because known active damage presents an immediate and ongoing loss exposure.

**H.2.44** Require evidence of completed repair before reconsidering a risk declined for roof condition. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because eligibility depends on the condition at the time of binding.

**H.2.45** Review the entire roof system rather than evaluating only the area identified by the applicant. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because localized defects may reflect broader deterioration.

**H.2.46** Refer a risk when the roof condition differs materially across separate roof sections. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because each section may present a distinct source of loss.

**H.2.47** Document the basis for any roof condition exception or referral. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because the underwriting file must explain how the condition was evaluated.

**H.2.48** Use consistent descriptions when recording roof concerns in the underwriting file. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because clear documentation supports later review and claim evaluation.

**H.2.49** Do not characterize an unverified roof as newly replaced, fully repaired, or free of defects. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because unsupported descriptions can create inaccurate eligibility records.

**H.2.50** Explain any condition requirement to the applicant in terms of the observed roof concern. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because clear communication helps obtain relevant repair evidence.

**H.2.51** Avoid directing the applicant to use a particular contractor or repair method. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because underwriting may identify an eligibility concern without controlling the repair decision.

**H.2.52** Review the roof payment floor when discussing how roof-related losses may be evaluated. This rule rests on the Roof Settlement provision because settlement considerations may differ from underwriting eligibility.

**H.2.53** Do not use anticipated claim payment treatment to override a roof eligibility decision. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because binding decisions must be based on the present condition of the property.

**H.2.54** Consider the insured-to-value threshold for replacement cost settlement when available valuation information raises a settlement concern. This rule rests on the Replacement Cost Settlement provision because adequate valuation may affect the basis for loss settlement.

**H.2.55** Do not treat valuation information as proof that the roof is in acceptable condition. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because property value does not establish roof serviceability.

**H.2.56** Refer a risk when the applicant cannot provide sufficient information to evaluate a reported roof concern. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because incomplete information prevents a reliable eligibility determination.

**H.2.57** You may proceed when reliable evidence supports that the roof is sound and any prior concern has been satisfactorily resolved. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because underwriting may rely on current, verifiable conditions.

**H.2.58** Escalate unusual roof construction, specialized surfacing, or conditions outside ordinary underwriting review. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because uncommon roof features may require additional expertise.

**H.2.59** Apply roof condition requirements consistently to renewals and new business. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because the exposure created by an impaired roof is not limited to a particular transaction type.

**H.2.60** Record the final roof decision and the evidence supporting it before binding or declining the risk. This rule rests on the Roof Condition provision because the file should demonstrate a clear and supportable underwriting rationale.

## H.3 — Wind and Hail

**H.3.1** You may consider wind and hail exposure when evaluating roof condition, exterior maintenance, and the surrounding property environment. This review rests on the Windstorm and Hail Coverage provision because those conditions affect the likelihood and severity of a covered loss.

**H.3.3** Refer wind mitigation inspections to an acceptable qualified inspector and retain the report in the underwriting file. This requirement rests on the Wind Mitigation Inspection provision because the report must support the characteristics used in rating and eligibility review.

**H.3.4** Review the inspection for evidence that roof-to-wall connections, roof geometry, and opening protection are consistent with the application. This review rests on the Wind Mitigation Inspection provision because favorable construction features must be verified before they affect underwriting treatment.

**H.3.5** Do not rely on applicant descriptions of wind-resistant features when the inspection identifies conflicting conditions. This rule rests on the Wind Mitigation Inspection provision because the inspection provides the more reliable basis for evaluating loss susceptibility.

**H.3.6** Refer risks with incomplete wind mitigation information before offering terms that depend on protective construction features. This requirement rests on the Wind Mitigation Inspection provision because incomplete information can produce unsupported eligibility or pricing decisions.

**H.3.7** You may bind a risk with ordinary wind exposure when the dwelling is maintained and no material roof concerns are present. This guidance rests on the Windstorm and Hail Coverage provision because ordinary weather exposure is contemplated when the property condition remains acceptable.

**H.3.8** Refer a dwelling with visible roof deterioration, missing materials, or unrepaired storm damage for underwriting review. This requirement rests on the Windstorm and Hail Coverage provision because deferred repairs can increase the chance of water intrusion following wind or hail.

**H.3.9** Do not bind a risk when the roof condition suggests an active leak or an unaddressed opening in the building envelope. This rule rests on the Windstorm and Hail Coverage provision because an existing source of damage is not an acceptable fortuity exposure.

**H.4.28** You should preserve photographs and physical evidence showing the drain, fixture, cleanout, or other alleged point of backup when practical. This follows the Duties After Loss provision, because the evidence may be necessary to distinguish backup from excluded water entry.

**H.4.36** You should avoid describing water backup coverage as broad protection for all water-related damage. This follows the Water Backup Endorsement exclusion, because coverage remains limited to the specifically described backup causes.

**H.4.37** You may bind the endorsement only when the submitted risk information does not reveal an unacceptable drainage, plumbing, or maintenance concern. This follows the Water Backup Endorsement underwriting intent, because accurate selection depends on the condition of the insured premises.

**H.4.38** You should refer unusual cause-of-loss facts for further review when the reported event may involve mixed sources of water or competing exclusions. This follows the Water Backup Endorsement insuring agreement, because a complete cause analysis is required before coverage can be confirmed.

## H.6 — Handling Procedures

**H.6.1** You should open a claim file when notice of a potentially covered loss is received. This preserves a reliable record of the information provided at intake.

**H.6.2** You should capture the reported date of loss, loss location, involved parties, and reported cause of damage. This supports an accurate coverage review and orderly investigation.

**H.6.3** You should record when the receipt acknowledgement period begins and route the notice without delay. This helps maintain a clear claim file and timely communication.

**H.6.4** You should provide the insured with the appropriate claim contact information and explain how to submit supporting materials. This reduces avoidable delays caused by incomplete communication.

**H.6.5** You should identify any immediate safety concern before discussing property damage in detail. This helps protect occupants and limits further loss.

**H.6.6** You must begin reasonable mitigation within 7 days after discovery of damage. This helps prevent avoidable deterioration and distinguishes ensuing damage from unrepaired conditions.

**H.6.7** You should advise the insured to retain damaged property when practical until it can be inspected. This allows the claim file to document cause, extent, and condition of the damage.

**H.6.8** You should request photographs, repair proposals, invoices, and other available loss documentation. This provides support for the scope of damage and the reasonableness of claimed costs.

**H.6.9** You should arrange an inspection when the reported facts cannot be confirmed from the available documentation. This helps establish the cause of loss before a coverage position is made.

**H.6.10** You should use a qualified field representative or specialist where the reported damage involves a technical issue. This improves the reliability of findings involving construction, systems, or causation.

**H.6.11** You should document all material observations from an inspection in the claim file. This preserves the basis for the handling decision if the file is later reviewed.

**H.6.12** You should distinguish between observed damage, reported damage, and damage that remains unverified. This prevents assumptions from being treated as established facts.

**H.6.13** You should evaluate whether the reported event is sudden, accidental, gradual, repeated, or maintenance-related. This helps determine whether the facts support coverage or indicate an excluded condition.

**H.6.14** You should identify any prior damage, repair history, or recurring condition that may affect the claim. This helps separate the current loss from preexisting property issues.

**H.6.15** You should review the claim for potential subrogation whenever another party may have caused or contributed to the loss. This protects the insurer’s recovery rights and preserves relevant evidence.

**H.6.17** You should consider whether emergency work was reasonable and necessary to protect the property. This supports appropriate handling of mitigation expenses without approving unrelated improvements.

**H.6.18** You should separate emergency stabilization work from permanent repair work in the claim file. This helps clarify which expenses addressed the immediate loss and which relate to restoration.

**H.6.19** You should request a sworn proof when the reported loss requires formal confirmation of claimed facts or amounts. This creates a clear record of the insured’s representations.

**H.6.21** You should evaluate each claimed item against the available loss evidence before issuing payment. This reduces the risk of paying for damage not caused by the reported event.

## H.7 — Authority Limits

**H.7.1** You may bind a risk only when its Coverage A amount falls within your authority. This keeps binding decisions aligned with the authority assigned to your role.

**H.7.2** Your Coverage A line authority is $750,000. This limit prevents a binding decision from exceeding the underwriting review assigned to you.

**H.7.3** Coverage A ceilings are set by the state exception ruleceilings are set by the state exception rule (see Personal Lines Underwriting Manual, R530 Rule 530 — New York State Exceptions). This control ensures that state-specific authority restrictions are applied consistently.

**H.7.4** Refer a risk when the requested Coverage A amount exceeds your authority. Referral allows the appropriate underwriting level to assess the exposure before binding.

**H.7.5** Do not reduce Coverage A solely to place a risk within your authority. A reduced amount may leave the dwelling inadequately protected and creates avoidable coverage concerns.

**H.7.6** Use the dwelling valuation accepted for the submission when applying your authority limit. This avoids using an unsupported value to obtain a binding result.

**H.7.7** Refer a risk when the valuation appears incomplete, inconsistent, or unsuitable for the dwelling. A reliable valuation is necessary because Coverage A is the foundation for the homeowners coverage structure.

**H.7.8** Do not bind when material property characteristics remain unresolved. Unresolved characteristics may affect the dwelling value, construction classification, or eligibility determination.

**H.7.9** Refer risks with unusual construction, specialized finishes, or features that are not adequately reflected in the valuation. These characteristics can materially change the cost to repair or replace the dwelling.

**H.7.10** Refer a risk when the reported replacement cost conflicts with the property description. Conflicting information can indicate that the requested Coverage A amount is not dependable.

**H.7.11** Do not rely on a prior insurer's limit as the sole support for the requested Coverage A amount. Prior limits may reflect different underwriting assumptions or incomplete property information.

**H.7.12** Refer a risk when the applicant requests a Coverage A amount that differs from the supported valuation. This allows review of whether the requested amount reasonably protects the insured dwelling.

**H.7.13** Do not bind a risk based on estimated values that lack adequate support. Unsupported estimates can produce an authority decision that does not reflect the actual exposure.

**H.7.14** Refer a risk when recent improvements may have changed the dwelling's replacement cost. Improvements can materially affect the amount of insurance needed for the property.

**H.7.15** Refer a risk when significant renovation activity is underway or planned. Construction activity can alter both the property hazard and the reliability of the reported valuation.
