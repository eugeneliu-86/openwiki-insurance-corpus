# Roof Claims and the ACV Schedule







## L.1 — Learning Objectives

**L.1.1** You will explain why roof claims often require careful review of both coverage and valuation. You will distinguish the cause of loss from the method used to value covered damage.

**L.1.2** You will describe actual cash value in clear, practical language. You will understand that actual cash value may reflect depreciation when the policy calls for it.

**L.1.3** You will recognize the purpose of an ACV schedule in roof claim handling. You will use the schedule as a tool for applying the policy’s valuation approach consistently.

**L.1.4** You will identify the information needed to evaluate roof age, materials, condition, and damage. You will understand why reliable claim facts support a fair valuation.

**L.1.5** You will separate pre-existing wear from damage caused by the reported event. You will avoid treating ordinary deterioration as new covered loss.

**L.1.6** You will evaluate whether observed damage is consistent with the reported cause of loss. You will know when the available evidence calls for additional investigation.

**L.1.7** You will understand how roof condition can affect an actual cash value estimate. You will document the observations that support your valuation decision.

**L.1.8** You will identify when repair may be appropriate and when replacement may be necessary. You will connect that decision to the physical damage rather than to a preferred outcome.

**L.1.9** You will review estimates for damaged materials, labor, and related work. You will look for scope items that are supported by the loss and remove items that are not.

**L.1.10** You will recognize that code-related work may require separate coverage analysis. You will avoid assuming that every required upgrade is included in the basic roof payment.

**L.1.11** You will communicate the difference between replacement cost and actual cash value without using confusing claim jargon. You will help the insured understand how the valuation method affects the payment.

**L.1.12** You will explain depreciation in a respectful and straightforward way. You will make clear that depreciation is based on the property involved and the applicable policy terms.

**L.1.13** You will avoid applying a schedule mechanically when claim facts do not fit the available categories. You will escalate questions when the roof type, condition, or valuation basis is unclear.

**L.1.14** You will identify common documentation that supports a roof claim decision. You will understand the value of inspection notes, photographs, estimates, weather information, and policy review.

**L.1.15** You will write claim notes that show how you reached your conclusion. You will record the facts reviewed, the reasoning applied, and any remaining questions.

**L.1.16** You will recognize when a contractor estimate may include work beyond the covered damage. You will address differences in scope professionally and with support from the file.

**L.1.17** You will understand that matching concerns may require careful policy and fact analysis. You will avoid promising a particular outcome before coverage has been confirmed.

**L.1.18** You will identify situations that may require assistance from a supervisor, specialist, or other claim resource. You will raise those issues early enough to avoid unnecessary delay.

**L.1.19** You will apply the ACV schedule consistently while remaining attentive to the individual claim facts. You will understand that consistency does not mean ignoring relevant evidence.

**L.1.20** You will communicate next steps clearly to insureds and claim partners. You will explain what information is needed and why it matters to the claim decision.

**L.1.21** You will recognize the importance of treating roof claims with fairness, accuracy, and empathy. You will balance efficient handling with the need for a well-supported evaluation.

**L.1.22** You will finish this material prepared to review roof claims using the applicable valuation approach. You will be able to explain your reasoning in language that is clear, professional, and grounded in the claim file.

## L.2 — Key Concepts

**L.2.1** **Q:** What is an ACV schedule in a roof claim? How should you use it during claim review?

**A:** An ACV schedule is a tool that helps determine the payable value of roof surfacing after age, condition, and other applicable factors are considered. You use it to support a consistent evaluation of the damaged roof surfacing.

**L.2.2** **Q:** Does an ACV schedule tell you whether the loss is covered? What question does it answer instead?

**A:** No. Coverage is decided by the cause of loss, the applicable coverage terms, exclusions, and the facts of the claim. The schedule helps value covered roof surfacing after coverage has been established.

**L.2.3** **Q:** What roof property is usually evaluated with an ACV schedule? Why is that distinction important?

**A:** The schedule usually applies to roof surfacing, such as shingles, tiles, panels, or similar exterior materials. You should not assume that every roof component receives the same scheduled treatment.

**L.2.4** **Q:** Why do you need to identify the damaged material carefully? What can go wrong if the material is described loosely?

**A:** Different roof materials can age and perform differently. A vague description can lead to an unsupported condition assessment or an incorrect valuation approach.

**L.2.5** **Q:** Is roof age important when applying an ACV schedule? How should you handle uncertain age information?

**A:** Roof age can affect the condition and expected remaining serviceability of the damaged surfacing. When age information is uncertain, document the available evidence and avoid treating an estimate as a confirmed fact.

**L.2.6** **Q:** What evidence can help establish roof age? What should you record from that evidence?

**A:** Useful evidence can include installation records, permits, invoices, inspection reports, photographs, and reliable statements from knowledgeable sources. Record where the information came from and whether it appears complete.

**L.2.7** **Q:** Can visible wear affect ACV evaluation? What type of observations are useful?

**A:** Yes. Wear may help explain the roof’s condition before the reported loss. Note observations such as granule loss, brittleness, curling, cracking, patching, staining, corrosion, or prior repairs.

**L.2.8** **Q:** Does visible wear automatically mean the reported damage is not covered? Why not?

**A:** No. Wear and deterioration may exist alongside damage from a covered cause of loss. You still need to separate preexisting condition from damage caused by the reported event.

**L.2.9** **Q:** What is the difference between damage and condition? Why should you keep those ideas separate?

**A:** Damage is physical change caused by an event or cause of loss. Condition describes the roof’s state before the reported event, including age-related wear, maintenance, and prior repairs.

**L.2.10** **Q:** Why are photographs important in a roof ACV review? What should the photographs show?

**A:** Photographs help support observations about material type, condition, damage, repairs, and overall roof appearance. They should be clear enough to connect the observed condition to the claim evaluation.

**L.2.11** **Q:** Should you rely only on close-up photographs? What other views can help?

**A:** No. Close-up images may show material details but may not show the condition across the roof. Wider views can help show patterns, slopes, transitions, repairs, and the relationship between damaged and undamaged areas.

**L.2.12** **Q:** Why should you note the roof slope or area where damage appears? How does that support the evaluation?

**A:** Damage and wear may not appear uniformly across all roof areas. Identifying the location helps explain why a particular repair scope or replacement scope is supported.

**L.2.13** **Q:** What should you do when the roof has more than one material type? Can you treat all materials as identical?

**A:** Evaluate each material type based on its own characteristics and observed condition. Do not assume that different materials have the same age, condition, or scheduled value.

**L.2.14** **Q:** How should you handle a roof with repaired areas? Why do prior repairs matter?

**A:** Identify repaired areas and determine whether they are related to the reported damage. Prior repairs can affect condition, appearance, material availability, and the scope needed to address covered damage.

**L.2.15** **Q:** Does a newer repair prove that the entire roof is newer? What is the better approach?

**A:** No. A newer repair may show only that a limited area received work. Evaluate the repair area separately from the surrounding surfacing when the evidence supports doing so.

**L.2.16** **Q:** What is a replacement cost estimate used for in an ACV review? What should it include?

**A:** A replacement cost estimate establishes the reasonable cost to repair or replace covered damaged property before the ACV adjustment is applied. It should reflect the supported scope of covered work.

**L.2.17** **Q:** Should you prepare a replacement estimate before applying the ACV schedule? Why is that order helpful?

**A:** Yes. Starting with a supported replacement estimate helps you apply the schedule to a clear repair or replacement basis. It also makes the claim calculation easier to explain.

**L.2.18** **Q:** What does it mean to apply the schedule to the supported scope? What should you avoid?

**A:** Apply the schedule only to property and work that are within the covered damage scope. Avoid using the schedule to reduce costs that are unrelated to roof surfacing valuation.

**L.2.19** **Q:** Can labor be affected differently from roof surfacing? How should you handle that issue?

**A:** Labor treatment depends on the applicable coverage terms and the schedule instructions. Separate labor from surfacing costs when the claim handling approach requires that distinction.

**L.2.20** **Q:** Can debris removal be affected differently from roof surfacing? What should you confirm?

**A:** Debris removal may have its own coverage treatment and may not follow the same valuation method as roof surfacing. Confirm how the applicable coverage terms address that cost.

**L.2.21** **Q:** What should you do with permit costs in a roof claim? Why should they be identified clearly?

**A:** Identify permit costs separately when they are part of the supported repair scope. Clear itemization helps prevent the schedule from being applied to costs that require different treatment.

**L.2.22** **Q:** How should you handle code-related work? What must be established before including it?

**A:** Code-related work should be included only when it is supported by the loss, the repair scope, and the applicable coverage terms. Document the source of the requirement rather than relying on a general assumption.

**L.2.23** **Q:** Can matching concerns change the ACV schedule calculation? What should you evaluate first?

**A:** Matching concerns may affect the repair scope, but they do not automatically change how roof surfacing is valued. Establish the supported scope before deciding how the schedule applies.

**L.2.24** **Q:** What if the insured requests full roof replacement? What is your role?

**A:** Review the request against the observed damage, material condition, repair feasibility, and applicable coverage terms. Explain the basis for the supported scope without treating a request as proof of necessity.

**L.2.25** **Q:** What if a contractor recommends replacement? Is that recommendation enough by itself?

**A:** A contractor recommendation can be useful claim information, but it is not the only evidence you should consider. Compare it with inspection findings, photographs, damage documentation, and the supported scope.

**L.2.26** **Q:** Why should you review contractor estimates carefully? What differences might you find?

**A:** Contractor estimates may include work that is unrelated to covered damage, condition issues, upgrades, or broad replacement recommendations. Identify what is supported, what needs clarification, and what may require separate treatment.

**L.2.27** **Q:** How should you respond when estimates differ? What is the goal of your review?

**A:** Compare the estimates by scope, material, quantity, labor, and included operations. The goal is to identify the supported cost of covered work, not simply to select the lowest or highest estimate.

**L.2.28** **Q:** What if the roof cannot be safely inspected? How should you document that limitation?

**A:** Use available evidence such as photographs, aerial imagery, reports, or inspection information obtained through safe methods. Clearly state the inspection limitation and avoid claiming observations that were not made.

**L.2.29** **Q:** Why should you avoid guessing about roof condition? What should you do instead?

**A:** Guessing can create an unsupported ACV adjustment and weaken the claim file. Use documented facts, explain reasonable inferences, and identify unresolved issues when evidence is limited.

**L.2.30** **Q:** What does a well-documented ACV explanation include? Who should be able to follow it?

**A:** It includes the damaged material, the supported repair or replacement scope, the condition evidence, and the schedule application. An insured, reviewer, or claim colleague should be able to follow the reasoning.

**L.2.31** **Q:** How should you explain ACV to an insured? What tone should you use?

**A:** Use plain language and explain that the payment reflects the covered damage after the scheduled valuation approach is applied. Be respectful, direct, and prepared to explain the evidence used in the evaluation.

**L.2.32** **Q:** Should you describe ACV as a penalty for an older roof? Why is that wording risky?

**A:** No. That wording can sound unfair and can misstate the purpose of the valuation approach. Explain that the evaluation considers the roof surfacing’s condition and applicable scheduled treatment.

**L.2.33** **Q:** What is the minimum payable percentage for roof surfacing? How should you apply it?

**A:** The schedule will not pay less than thirty (30) percent of roof surfacing replacement cost. Apply that floor only within the supported roof surfacing valuation calculation.

**L.2.34** **Q:** Does the minimum payable percentage decide whether a loss is covered? What does it address?

**A:** No. It addresses the payable value of roof surfacing within the scheduled valuation process. Coverage still depends on the reported cause of loss and the applicable coverage terms.

**L.2.35** **Q:** Does the minimum payable percentage apply to every cost on an estimate? What should you separate?

**A:** Not necessarily. Identify roof surfacing costs separately from costs that may receive different treatment. Clear estimating supports accurate application of the schedule.

**L.2.36** **Q:** What should you do if an estimate combines surfacing, labor, and unrelated work? Why is separation important?

**A:** Break out the estimate into understandable components before applying the schedule. Separation helps ensure that the valuation method is applied only where appropriate.

**L.2.37** **Q:** Can interior damage be handled through the roof ACV schedule? What is the better approach?

**A:** Interior damage should be evaluated under the applicable coverage treatment for that damaged property. Do not assume that a roof surfacing schedule applies to interior repairs.

**L.2.38** **Q:** Can gutters and downspouts be handled through the roof ACV schedule? What should you verify?

**A:** Gutters and downspouts may require separate evaluation because they are not necessarily roof surfacing. Verify the material, damage scope, and applicable valuation treatment.

**L.2.39** **Q:** Can roof decking be handled the same way as shingles? Why should you be careful?

**A:** Roof decking is a different component from roof surfacing and may require separate analysis. Do not extend the surfacing schedule to decking without support.

**L.2.40** **Q:** Can flashing be handled the same way as roof surfacing? What should guide your decision?

**A:** Flashing should be evaluated based on its material, damage, and applicable valuation treatment. Its presence within a roof repair does not automatically make it scheduled roof surfacing.

**L.2.41** **Q:** Why does causation still matter after you identify roof wear? What must you connect?

**A:** You must connect the claimed damage to the reported cause of loss. Evidence of wear may be relevant, but it does not replace a causation analysis.

**L.2.42** **Q:** What if the roof has both covered damage and maintenance issues? How should you communicate that result?

**A:** Separate the covered damage from conditions that are not part of the covered loss. Explain the distinction using observations and claim evidence rather than broad conclusions.

**L.2.43** **Q:** What if a damaged area was already leaking before the reported event? What should you investigate?

**A:** Investigate the condition before the reported event, the source of moisture, prior repairs, and evidence of new damage. Avoid assuming that all observed water damage came from the reported loss.

**L.2.44** **Q:** Why should you document conversations with the insured? What should the note capture?

**A:** Conversation notes can clarify reported loss details, roof history, repairs, and information provided by the insured. Capture relevant statements accurately and distinguish them from confirmed observations.

**L.2.45** **Q:** Why should you document conversations with contractors? How should you treat their opinions?

**A:** Contractor discussions can help explain scope recommendations and material concerns. Record their observations and reasoning, then evaluate those opinions against other claim evidence.

**L.2.46** **Q:** What should you do when the evidence conflicts? How can you keep the file fair?

**A:** Identify the conflict, seek clarification where practical, and explain which evidence supports the final evaluation. Do not ignore evidence simply because it points toward a different conclusion.

**L.2.47** **Q:** How should you handle unavailable material concerns? What must be shown?

**A:** Confirm the material issue and determine whether it affects the supported repair scope. Do not assume unavailability requires broader replacement without reliable evidence.

**L.2.48** **Q:** Why does repair feasibility matter in a roof claim? What should your file explain?

**A:** Repair feasibility can affect whether localized work addresses the covered damage. Your file should explain the practical basis for the repair or replacement scope selected.

**L.2.49** **Q:** What if roof damage appears limited but the insured reports widespread problems? How should you proceed?

**A:** Consider the reported concerns, then compare them with inspection findings and available evidence. Explain whether the evidence supports damage beyond the observed area.

**L.2.50** **Q:** What makes an ACV calculation easy to audit? What should be visible in the claim file?

**A:** The file should show the supported replacement scope, the relevant roof surfacing cost, the schedule application, and the resulting payment basis. Clear notes and itemized estimates make the calculation easier to review.

**L.2.51** **Q:** Why should you avoid unexplained adjustments? What is the risk?

**A:** An unexplained adjustment can appear arbitrary and may be difficult to defend. State what was adjusted, why it was adjusted, and what evidence supports the decision.

**L.2.52** **Q:** Can you apply the schedule before confirming the damaged material? Why is that a problem?

**A:** No. The material type is central to evaluating the proper scope and scheduled treatment. Applying the schedule too early can produce an inaccurate result.

**L.2.53** **Q:** What should you do if the schedule result seems inconsistent with the facts? What should you review?

**A:** Review the material identification, condition information, replacement estimate, and schedule inputs. Correct any unsupported assumption before communicating the payment decision.

**L.2.54** **Q:** How can you keep your explanation understandable without oversimplifying it? What details matter most?

**A:** Focus on the damaged material, observed condition, covered scope, and valuation result. Use plain language while keeping the explanation tied to documented facts.

**L.2.55** **Q:** What is the main purpose of a roof ACV schedule? How does it help claim handling?

**A:** Its purpose is to provide a consistent method for valuing covered roof surfacing based on the scheduled approach. It helps you explain how the payable value was reached.

**L.2.56** **Q:** What is the best final check before issuing a roof ACV payment? What should you confirm?

**A:** Confirm that coverage, causation, scope, material identification, condition evidence, estimate support, and schedule application are clearly documented. Make sure your explanation matches the calculation and the facts in the claim file.

## L.3 — Worked Examples

**L.3.1** **Q:** What is the starting point for a roof claim? **A:** Begin with the reported cause of loss and the damaged roof materials. Confirm that the policy provides coverage before applying the ACV schedule.

**L.3.2** **Q:** When does the roof ACV schedule matter? **A:** The schedule matters when covered roof damage involves surfacing subject to scheduled valuation. It may change the settlement amount even when coverage exists.

**L.3.3** **Q:** How should you identify the damaged property? **A:** Separate roof surfacing from decking, framing, gutters, and related components. Apply the correct valuation approach to the property actually damaged.

**L.3.4** **Q:** What should you review before discussing payment? **A:** Review the declarations, endorsements, cause of loss, and inspection findings. A clear coverage review prevents a valuation discussion from becoming misleading.

**L.3.5** **Q:** Does visible roof damage automatically mean payment is owed? **A:** No. The damage must result from a covered cause of loss and must exceed any applicable deductible.

**L.3.6** **Q:** What if the insured reports a leak but no recent event? **A:** Ask when the leak was discovered and inspect for signs of a covered event. Long-term deterioration may not be covered simply because water entered the building.

**L.3.7** **Q:** How do you handle wind damage to roof surfacing? **A:** Confirm that wind caused direct physical damage to the surfacing. Then determine whether the roof ACV schedule applies to that damaged material.

**L.3.8** **Q:** How do you handle hail damage to roof surfacing? **A:** Document the hail impact and distinguish it from wear, manufacturing defects, or installation concerns. Use the inspection findings to support the coverage decision.

**L.3.9** **Q:** What if the roof was already in poor condition? **A:** Preexisting condition does not automatically remove coverage for new covered damage. It does matter when you separate storm damage from unrelated deterioration.

**L.3.10** **Q:** Can you assume every marked shingle was damaged by the reported event? **A:** No. Compare the alleged damage with the event facts, weather information, and inspection observations.

**L.3.11** **Q:** What is the best way to explain ACV to an insured? **A:** Explain that the schedule may value eligible roof surfacing based on policy terms rather than replacement cost. Avoid promising a payment amount before the claim review is complete.

**L.3.12** **Q:** Should you describe the roof schedule as a penalty? **A:** No. Describe it as a policy valuation provision that may apply to eligible roof surfacing.

**L.3.13** **Q:** What roof information should you obtain early? **A:** Obtain the roof material, installation history, repairs, prior losses, and available maintenance records. These details help you evaluate the claim accurately.

**L.3.14** **Q:** Why does roof material matter? **A:** Different materials may have different coverage terms and valuation treatment. Identify the actual damaged surfacing rather than relying only on the insured's description.

**L.3.15** **Q:** What if more than one roof material is present? **A:** Identify which material was damaged and which policy provision applies to it. Do not treat the entire roof as a single material without support.

**L.3.16** **Q:** How should you address a roof with prior repairs? **A:** Determine whether the repaired area was damaged by the reported event. Prior repair work may help explain conditions that are not new damage.

**L.3.17** **Q:** What if the insured cannot provide roof installation records? **A:** Use available evidence such as permits, invoices, inspection reports, photos, and credible statements. Document the basis for your conclusion.

**L.3.18** **Q:** Can contractor statements determine coverage by themselves? **A:** No. Consider contractor information with the policy terms, inspection findings, and loss facts.

**L.3.19** **Q:** What if a contractor recommends full replacement? **A:** Review the recommendation, but independently determine the scope of covered damage. A replacement recommendation is not by itself proof of covered loss.

**L.3.20** **Q:** How should you handle a request for matching materials? **A:** Identify the damaged material and review the applicable policy language. Explain that matching concerns do not automatically establish coverage for undamaged property.

**L.3.21** **Q:** Does an ordinance requirement automatically expand roof coverage? **A:** No. Review the policy provisions that address ordinance or law exposure. Coverage may depend on the circumstances and policy terms.

**L.3.22** **Q:** What if code requirements affect the repair method? **A:** Separate the covered direct physical damage from any code-related cost. Then apply the relevant policy language to each part of the estimate.

**L.3.23** **Q:** How should you treat roof decking damage? **A:** Inspect the decking separately from the roof surfacing. Its valuation treatment may differ from the surfacing schedule.

**L.3.24** **Q:** What if water damages the interior after roof damage? **A:** Determine whether a covered event created an opening that allowed water to enter. Document the path of water and the condition of the roof.

**L.3.25** **Q:** Is every interior stain evidence of a covered roof leak? **A:** No. Staining can result from condensation, plumbing, maintenance issues, or long-term seepage.

**L.3.26** **Q:** What if the insured made temporary repairs? **A:** Review the repairs and retain invoices, photos, and damaged materials when available. Reasonable mitigation can help prevent additional damage.

**L.3.27** **Q:** Should temporary repairs be ignored because they changed the scene? **A:** No. Ask for photos and repair records, then evaluate the available evidence. The claim can still be investigated after mitigation work.

**L.3.28** **Q:** How should you document roof damage? **A:** Use photos, diagrams, measurements, inspection notes, and a clear explanation of observed conditions. Good documentation should support both coverage and valuation.

**L.3.29** **Q:** Why should you photograph undamaged areas? **A:** Undamaged areas help show the extent and pattern of loss. They can also support the distinction between event damage and general wear.

**L.3.30** **Q:** What if the damage pattern is inconsistent with the reported cause? **A:** Ask additional questions and obtain further inspection support if needed. Do not force the facts to fit the reported cause of loss.

**L.3.31** **Q:** How should you discuss deductibles? **A:** Explain that the applicable deductible is applied after covered loss is evaluated under the policy. Confirm which deductible applies before communicating the settlement.

**L.3.32** **Q:** What if the insured asks about the all-other-perils deductible minimum? **A:** Explain that deductible application depends on the policy and the cause of loss. Do not estimate the effect without reviewing the declarations and endorsements.

**L.3.33** **Q:** What if the loss followed a named storm? **A:** Confirm the event classification and review the applicable deductible provisions. The named storm deductible minimum may be relevant to the claim review.

**L.3.34** **Q:** Can a deductible be selected because it produces a simpler outcome? **A:** No. Apply the deductible required by the policy and supported by the loss facts.

**L.3.35** **Q:** What is the right order for a roof claim analysis? **A:** Start with coverage, then identify damaged property, determine valuation, and apply the deductible. This sequence helps keep the file organized.

**L.3.36** **Q:** What if the insured focuses only on replacement cost? **A:** Acknowledge the concern and explain that the policy controls the valuation method. Keep the discussion focused on the damaged property and applicable terms.

**L.3.37** **Q:** Should you tell the insured that every old roof receives ACV treatment? **A:** No. Review the actual policy language and the roof information before making that statement.

**L.3.38** **Q:** How do you determine whether the schedule applies by roof age? **A:** The roof ACV schedule applies when roof surfacing age reaches twelve (12) years. Document the evidence used to establish the roof surfacing age.

**L.3.39** **Q:** What if the age evidence is incomplete? **A:** Request available records and evaluate credible supporting information. Clearly document any uncertainty that remains.

**L.3.40** **Q:** Can you rely only on a homeowner's estimate of roof age? **A:** Treat it as relevant information, but look for support when possible. The file should show why you accepted or questioned the estimate.

**L.3.41** **Q:** What if records conflict about when the roof was installed? **A:** Compare the sources and request clarification where practical. Use the most reliable available evidence and explain your reasoning.

**L.3.42** **Q:** How should you address a roof that was partially replaced? **A:** Identify the damaged area and determine whether its installation history differs from the rest of the roof. Avoid assuming that all sections share the same age.

**L.3.43** **Q:** What if a roof overlay is present? **A:** Identify the damaged layer and review the policy language carefully. An overlay can complicate both inspection and valuation.

**L.3.44** **Q:** Does maintenance history matter? **A:** Yes. Maintenance records may help distinguish sudden covered damage from conditions that developed over time.

**L.3.45** **Q:** What if the roof has algae, granule loss, or curling? **A:** Document those conditions separately from alleged event damage. They may indicate wear or deterioration rather than direct physical loss.

**L.3.46** **Q:** How should you address brittle or fragile materials? **A:** Focus on whether the reported event caused direct physical damage. Do not assume that breakage establishes coverage without considering the condition of the material.

**L.3.47** **Q:** What if the insured says repairs cannot be completed without replacing a larger area? **A:** Review the repair reasoning and applicable policy provisions. Separate practical construction concerns from covered damage determination.

**L.3.48** **Q:** How do you handle disputed scope? **A:** Explain what the inspection supports and invite relevant additional documentation. Keep the discussion tied to observed damage and policy language.

**L.3.49** **Q:** What if the insured submits a higher contractor estimate? **A:** Compare the estimate line by line with the supported scope. Identify differences in material, labor, repairs, and noncovered work.

**L.3.50** **Q:** Should you reject a contractor estimate without explanation? **A:** No. Explain the supported scope and the basis for any differences. Clear communication reduces avoidable disputes.

**L.3.51** **Q:** What if the contractor includes upgrades? **A:** Separate upgrade costs from the repair or replacement of covered damage. Explain that betterment may not be part of the covered settlement.

**L.3.52** **Q:** How should you treat undamaged gutters? **A:** Do not include them merely because roof work is occurring. Confirm direct physical damage and applicable coverage.

**L.3.53** **Q:** What if flashing is damaged with the roof surfacing? **A:** Inspect the flashing and document whether it sustained covered damage. Its valuation may require separate consideration.

**L.3.54** **Q:** How should you handle vents damaged during a storm? **A:** Determine whether the storm caused direct physical damage to the vents. Include only the supported repair scope.

**L.3.55** **Q:** What if debris removal is needed? **A:** Review whether debris removal is covered and connected to the reported loss. Document the debris source and related work.

**L.3.56** **Q:** Can emergency tarping be covered? **A:** Review the policy terms and the reason the tarp was needed. Document how the work protected covered property from further damage.

**L.3.57** **Q:** What if the roof was leaking before the reported event? **A:** Investigate whether the event caused new damage or worsened an existing condition. The timing and condition evidence are important.

**L.3.58** **Q:** How should you discuss wear and tear? **A:** Explain that wear and tear is different from sudden direct physical damage. Use inspection findings rather than broad conclusions.

**L.3.59** **Q:** What if the insured says the storm exposed a hidden defect? **A:** Determine what damage the storm actually caused. A covered event does not necessarily make every discovered defect covered.

**L.3.60** **Q:** What if damage is limited to cosmetic marks? **A:** Review the policy and determine whether the marks constitute covered direct physical damage. Document their effect on the roof material.

**L.3.61** **Q:** How should you approach hail bruising allegations? **A:** Evaluate the surface condition, damage pattern, and available event evidence. Avoid relying on appearance alone.

**L.3.62** **Q:** What if the claim involves wind-driven rain? **A:** Determine whether covered damage created an opening that allowed the water intrusion. Inspect both exterior and interior conditions.

**L.3.63** **Q:** Can interior damage prove a roof opening existed? **A:** Not by itself. Interior damage may support the investigation, but the roof condition must also be evaluated.

**L.3.64** **Q:** What if a roofer cannot find storm damage but recommends replacement? **A:** Document the recommendation and the inspection findings. The recommendation does not replace a coverage analysis.

**L.3.65** **Q:** How should you handle an engineer's report? **A:** Review the report for its observations, reasoning, and conclusions. Compare it with the policy terms and other file evidence.

**L.3.66** **Q:** What if expert opinions differ? **A:** Identify the factual basis for each opinion and seek clarification when needed. Document why the final claim decision is supported.

**L.3.67** **Q:** When should you request additional inspection support? **A:** Request it when material facts remain unclear or technical issues affect coverage. Use specialists to resolve evidence gaps, not to avoid making a decision.

**L.3.68** **Q:** How should you explain a partial payment? **A:** State what damage is covered, how it was valued, and what deductible applies. Be clear about any remaining issues under review.

**L.3.69** **Q:** What if the insured asks why depreciation is not shown in the usual way? **A:** Explain that a roof ACV schedule may use policy-based valuation for eligible surfacing. Avoid comparing methods unless the policy language supports the comparison.

**L.3.70** **Q:** Is the roof schedule the same as a general depreciation calculation? **A:** Not necessarily. Follow the policy valuation language that applies to the damaged roof surfacing.

**L.3.71** **Q:** What if only a small roof area is damaged? **A:** Evaluate the actual extent of covered damage. Do not enlarge the scope without support from the policy or evidence.

**L.3.72** **Q:** What if repair is not feasible for the damaged material? **A:** Document why repair is not feasible and review the supported replacement scope. Feasibility should be based on evidence, not preference.

**L.3.73** **Q:** How should you handle discontinued roofing material? **A:** Confirm the material condition and available repair options. Review policy language before promising replacement of undamaged areas.

**L.3.74** **Q:** What if the insured raises market availability concerns? **A:** Record the concern and review the information provided. Availability alone does not determine the covered scope.

**L.3.75** **Q:** Does the roof schedule apply to interior finishes? **A:** Evaluate interior finishes under the policy provisions applicable to those materials. Do not extend the roof surfacing schedule beyond its terms.

**L.3.76** **Q:** How should you handle damage to solar equipment on a roof? **A:** Identify the equipment separately from roof surfacing. Review the policy treatment for the equipment and the cause of loss.

**L.3.77** **Q:** What if rooftop equipment must be moved for repair work? **A:** Determine whether the movement cost is related to covered damage and supported by policy terms. Separate access costs from unrelated improvements.

**L.3.78** **Q:** How do you handle a detached structure roof claim? **A:** Confirm the structure's coverage and identify the damaged roof material. Then apply the relevant valuation provisions.

**L.3.79** **Q:** What if the damaged roof belongs to a business-use structure? **A:** Review the policy's treatment of the structure and its use. Do not assume the dwelling analysis applies unchanged.

**L.3.80** **Q:** How should you respond to a claim involving theft of roof materials? **A:** Confirm the cause of loss, ownership, and timing of the alleged theft. Keep this analysis separate from unrelated coverage topics such as the precious stones theft limit.

**L.3.81** **Q:** What if roof damage occurs with water backup? **A:** Separate roof-related water entry from water that originated through drains, sewers, or sump systems. The water backup and sump overflow sublimit may be relevant to that separate analysis.

**L.3.82** **Q:** Should you apply coinsurance concepts automatically to a roof claim? **A:** No. Review the policy and determine whether the coinsurance threshold is relevant to the claim. Do not introduce it when it does not apply.

**L.3.83** **Q:** What if the insured believes all roof-related costs are covered? **A:** Explain that coverage depends on the damaged property, cause of loss, exclusions, conditions, and valuation terms. Use plain language and avoid overpromising.

**L.3.84** **Q:** How should you explain an exclusion? **A:** State the exclusion clearly and connect it to the observed facts. Avoid broad statements that go beyond the policy wording.

**L.3.85** **Q:** What if covered and excluded causes both contributed to damage? **A:** Review the policy language and facts carefully. Document the evidence supporting the claim decision.

**L.3.86** **Q:** How do you handle late reporting? **A:** Ask whether the delay affected the ability to inspect the loss or determine its cause. Apply policy conditions based on the actual facts.

**L.3.87** **Q:** What if repairs began before inspection? **A:** Obtain photos, invoices, material samples, and contractor information when available. Evaluate whether enough evidence remains to determine coverage.

**L.3.88** **Q:** Should you ask for prior claim history? **A:** Yes. Prior losses and repairs may help identify preexisting damage or repeated conditions.

**L.3.89** **Q:** What if the insured disagrees with the roof age finding? **A:** Explain the evidence used and invite relevant records that may change the analysis. Keep the conversation focused on documented facts.

**L.3.90** **Q:** How should you record your roof age conclusion? **A:** Identify the sources reviewed and summarize why they support the conclusion. Make the file understandable to someone who was not present.

**L.3.91** **Q:** What if the roof has been maintained exceptionally well? **A:** Good maintenance may be relevant to condition, but it does not replace the policy valuation terms. Consider maintenance evidence fairly.

**L.3.92** **Q:** Can a newer repair reset the age of the whole roof? **A:** Not automatically. Identify the specific area affected by the repair and its relationship to the damaged surfacing.

**L.3.93** **Q:** What if the insured has no photos from before the event? **A:** Use other available evidence, including inspection observations and third-party records. Lack of prior photos does not end the investigation.

**L.3.94** **Q:** How should you handle a claim with limited access to the roof? **A:** Use safe inspection methods and obtain appropriate support if access is not practical. Document any inspection limitations.

**L.3.95** **Q:** What if the roof is unsafe to inspect? **A:** Do not compromise safety. Arrange a suitable inspection approach and record why direct access was limited.

**L.3.96** **Q:** How should you communicate with a public adjuster? **A:** Treat the representative professionally and keep communications tied to the policy and evidence. Confirm authority to discuss the claim where needed.

**L.3.97** **Q:** What if the insured requests an advance payment? **A:** Review whether a covered amount can be established from the available information. Explain what evidence supports any payment decision.

**L.3.98** **Q:** How do you close a roof claim file well? **A:** Make sure the file explains the cause of loss, damage scope, valuation method, deductible, and communications. A complete file should show how the decision was reached.

**L.3.99** **Q:** What should a denial explanation include? **A:** State the relevant facts, policy basis, and information considered. Use respectful language and avoid conclusions that the evidence does not support.

**L.3.100** **Q:** What should a reservation communication accomplish? **A:** Explain what remains under review and why further information is needed. Do not suggest that coverage is resolved before the investigation supports that conclusion.

**L.3.101** **Q:** What if the insured asks for a final answer during the inspection? **A:** Explain that the inspection is part of the claim review and that the policy must be applied to the facts. Avoid making an immediate commitment.

**L.3.102** **Q:** How should you handle pressure to settle quickly? **A:** Work promptly, but do not skip coverage or valuation steps. A fast decision still needs a documented basis.

**L.3.103** **Q:** What if a claim involves several causes of loss? **A:** Analyze each cause separately and identify the damage associated with it. This prevents unrelated conditions from being included in the settlement.

**L.3.104** **Q:** How can you make the settlement explanation easier to understand? **A:** Use a clear sequence: covered damage, valuation method, deductible, and payment result. Invite questions about the specific claim facts.

**L.3.105** **Q:** What is the most important habit in roof ACV claims? **A:** Tie every conclusion to the policy language and documented evidence. Clear reasoning helps you make fair, consistent decisions.

## L.4 — Common Mistakes

**L.4.1 Q:** Can you treat every roof loss as a replacement cost claim? Does the ACV schedule always control the payment?

**L.4.2 A:** No. You must identify the applicable settlement approach before calculating the loss. Do not assume that roof damage alone answers that question.

**L.4.3 Q:** Can you start with the contractor’s estimate and call it the covered amount? Does the estimate settle coverage questions?

**L.4.4 A:** No. An estimate can help describe repairs, but it does not decide what is covered. Confirm the damaged property, cause of loss, and settlement method before relying on pricing.

**L.4.5 Q:** Is roof age enough to decide the ACV amount? Can you apply a schedule without checking the roof material?

**L.4.6 A:** No. Roof age is only part of the analysis when an ACV schedule applies. You also need to identify the roof surfacing that was damaged.

**L.4.7 Q:** Can you use the age of the home as the age of the roof? Is that always a reliable shortcut?

**L.4.8 A:** No. The roof may have been replaced, repaired, or altered after the home was built. Ask for reliable information about the roof itself.

**L.4.9 Q:** Can you use the installation date without checking whether it applies to the damaged roof area? Does every roof section necessarily have the same age?

**L.4.10 A:** No. Different roof areas may have different installation histories. Match the age information to the damaged surfacing whenever the facts support that distinction.

**L.4.11 Q:** Can you classify all shingles as the same roof material? Does a familiar appearance settle the material issue?

**L.4.12 A:** No. Similar-looking products can have different characteristics and settlement treatment. Document the material rather than relying on a casual description.

**L.4.13 Q:** Can you apply the schedule before confirming that the loss involves roof surfacing? Does every item near the roof receive the same treatment?

**L.4.14 A:** No. Roof surfacing, decking, framing, gutters, and interior damage can require separate analysis. Identify each damaged component and apply the applicable terms to that component.

**L.4.15 Q:** Can you assume that visible staining proves wind damage? Does appearance alone establish the cause of loss?

**L.4.16 A:** No. Staining can result from many conditions that are unrelated to a covered event. Compare the observed damage with the reported cause and the available evidence.

**L.4.17 Q:** Can you treat wear as sudden physical damage? Does deterioration become covered because it is discovered after a storm?

**L.4.18 A:** No. The timing of discovery does not establish the timing or cause of damage. Separate storm-created damage from conditions that developed over time.

**L.4.19 Q:** Can you include preexisting damage in the current loss estimate? Does the claimant’s repair need make all conditions part of the claim?

**L.4.20 A:** No. Pay attention to what the reported event caused. Document unrelated damage so it is not mistakenly included in the settlement.

**L.4.21 Q:** Can you deny a claim because the roof had prior repairs? Do repairs automatically prove that current damage is unrelated?

**L.4.22 A:** No. Prior repairs may explain a condition, but they do not end the investigation. Determine whether the reported event caused new covered damage.

**L.4.23 Q:** Can you rely only on aerial imagery for the roof inspection? Does imagery replace a careful damage evaluation?

**L.4.24 A:** No. Imagery can support the file, but it may not show condition, material, or damage details clearly. Use it with other reliable claim information.

**L.4.25 Q:** Can you accept a contractor’s statement about roof age without support? Is a confident statement the same as verified information?

**L.4.26 A:** No. Ask for records, invoices, permits, photographs, or other dependable support when available. Explain in the file how you reached the roof-age conclusion.

**L.4.27 Q:** Can you treat the entire roof as damaged because repairs may require matching work? Does matching automatically expand the covered damage area?

**L.4.28 A:** No. Start with the area that sustained covered direct physical damage. Consider matching concerns only after you identify the applicable coverage and required repair scope.

**L.4.29 Q:** Can you apply the ACV schedule to interior water damage without separate analysis? Does water entering through the roof make all damaged property roof surfacing?

**L.4.30 A:** No. Interior finishes and personal property are not roof surfacing. Evaluate each category of damaged property under its own applicable settlement terms.

**L.4.31 Q:** Can you overlook temporary protection costs while focusing on replacement pricing? Are reasonable emergency measures always part of the roof surfacing calculation?

**L.4.32 A:** No. Temporary protection and permanent repairs may require different handling. Identify the work performed and determine how the applicable terms treat it.

**L.4.33 Q:** Can you use current replacement pricing without checking the estimate scope? Does a larger estimate necessarily reflect a better repair plan?

**L.4.34 A:** No. Pricing must match the covered repair scope and the correct material. Review quantities, labor, waste, accessories, and included work for accuracy.

**L.4.35 Q:** Can you omit underlayment, flashing, or vents from the review? Are these items automatically included whenever roof surfacing is damaged?

**L.4.36 A:** No. Each item needs a coverage and damage analysis. Do not include or exclude an item merely because it is commonly associated with roofing work.

**L.4.37 Q:** Can you assume code-related work is covered whenever a contractor includes it? Does a building requirement decide coverage by itself?

**L.4.38 A:** No. Confirm whether the requirement applies and whether the applicable terms respond to the added cost. Keep code-related work separate from direct damage repairs during your review.

**L.4.39 Q:** Can you treat ordinance or law coverage as a percentage of Coverage A as part of the ACV schedule? Does that concept determine roof surfacing settlement?

**L.4.40 A:** No. Ordinance or law coverage as a percentage of Coverage A addresses a separate coverage issue. Do not use it to change the roof settlement method.

**L.4.41 Q:** Can you use the assessment coverage limit to support a roof payment? Does that limit describe roof damage coverage?

**L.4.42 A:** No. The assessment coverage limit is a separate concept. Keep it out of the roof ACV calculation unless the claim facts specifically require separate consideration.

**L.4.43 Q:** Can you include microbial remediation limit in the roof payment without analysis? Does moisture-related damage automatically make remediation part of the roof estimate?

**L.4.44 A:** No. The microbial remediation limit addresses a separate coverage concern. Investigate the condition and apply the applicable terms without folding it into roof surfacing damage.

**L.4.45 Q:** Can you delay the file because the sworn proof deadline has not arrived? Does that deadline remove the need for timely investigation?

**L.4.46 A:** No. The sworn proof deadline is important, but it does not replace active claim handling. Continue gathering facts and communicate clearly about needed information.

**L.4.47 Q:** Can you tell a claimant that the years within which an action against the insurer must be brought decides the roof payment? Does that concept establish the ACV amount?

**L.4.48 A:** No. The years within which an action against the insurer must be brought concern a separate claim condition. Do not confuse that condition with the settlement calculation.

**L.4.49 Q:** Can you use the nonrenewal notice period as a reason to change the roof settlement? Does renewal status determine whether the ACV schedule applies?

**L.4.50 A:** No. The nonrenewal notice period is separate from the claim settlement analysis. Focus on the coverage, damage, and applicable roof terms.

**L.4.51 Q:** Can you apply depreciation to labor without checking the applicable terms? Is labor treated the same way in every roof claim?

**L.4.52 A:** No. Do not make a blanket assumption about labor treatment. Confirm the applicable settlement language before calculating the payment.

**L.4.53 Q:** Can you use an ACV schedule as an extra deduction after calculating actual cash value? Does the schedule always operate as a separate reduction?

**L.4.54 A:** No. Follow the settlement method as written rather than stacking deductions. Your file should show how the applicable method produced the result.

**L.4.55 Q:** Can you say that depreciation and an ACV schedule mean the same thing? Will that shortcut always produce the correct explanation?

**L.4.56 A:** No. These concepts can operate differently depending on the applicable terms. Use clear language that describes the actual settlement method used.

**L.4.57 Q:** Can you explain the payment only by saying that the roof is old? Does that give the claimant enough information to understand the result?

**L.4.58 A:** No. Explain the covered damage, the identified roof material, and the applicable settlement approach. A clear explanation reduces confusion and supports the file.

**L.4.59 Q:** Can you ignore the deductible until after the roof payment is issued? Does the schedule replace the deductible analysis?

**L.4.60 A:** No. Apply the deductible as required by the applicable terms. Keep the deductible calculation distinct from the roof settlement calculation.

**L.4.61 Q:** Can you apply a special deductible without confirming that its trigger occurred? Does any weather report establish the required event?

**L.4.62 A:** No. Verify the event facts and the applicable deductible terms. Document why the selected deductible applies.

**L.4.63 Q:** Can you accept a repair recommendation without reviewing whether repair is feasible? Does replacement become necessary because it is preferred?

**L.4.64 A:** No. Consider whether the covered damage can be repaired in a workmanlike manner. Support the selected scope with facts, photographs, and expert input when needed.

**L.4.65 Q:** Can you require full replacement because some shingles are damaged? Does any damaged shingle prove that repair is impossible?

**L.4.66 A:** No. The damage evaluation should address the actual repairability of the affected area. Avoid broad conclusions that are not supported by the inspection findings.

**L.4.67 Q:** Can you disregard photographs because an estimate is detailed? Does a written estimate replace damage documentation?

**L.4.68 A:** No. Photographs help connect the estimate to observed conditions. Use clear images and notes to show what was damaged and why the scope is appropriate.

**L.4.69 Q:** Can you rely on photographs without recording where the damage was found? Do unlabeled images explain the repair scope?

**L.4.70 A:** No. Good documentation links images to roof areas and observed conditions. Your notes should let another reviewer follow the reasoning.

**L.4.71 Q:** Can you assume a leak proves an opening was created by the reported event? Does water intrusion identify the cause by itself?

**L.4.72 A:** No. Water can enter through many conditions, including preexisting defects. Investigate the path of entry and the cause of the opening.

**L.4.73 Q:** Can you treat a claim denial as complete without explaining the factual basis? Does a conclusion alone show how you reached it?

**L.4.74 A:** No. State the relevant facts and the coverage basis clearly. A well-supported explanation is fairer to the claimant and easier to review.

**L.4.75 Q:** Can you change the settlement approach after payment without documenting why? Does a revised estimate speak for itself?

**L.4.76 A:** No. Record what new information changed the analysis. Explain how that information affects the covered scope or settlement method.

**L.4.77 Q:** Can you treat contractor supplements as automatically payable? Does a supplement establish that added work is covered?

**L.4.78 A:** No. Review each requested item for damage, necessity, pricing, and coverage. Respond with a clear explanation when an item is not supported.

**L.4.79 Q:** Can you assume the claimant understands ACV terminology? Does using abbreviations make the explanation clearer?

**L.4.80 A:** No. Define your terms in plain language and connect them to the claim facts. Avoid unexplained abbreviations in claimant communications.

**L.4.81 Q:** Can you promise a final payment before the investigation is complete? Does an early estimate bind the claim outcome?

**L.4.82 A:** No. Be clear about what remains under review. Provide accurate updates without making commitments that the facts do not support.

**L.4.83 Q:** Can you overlook disputed facts because the estimated difference seems small? Does a limited dispute require less careful analysis?

**L.4.84 A:** No. Resolve material differences in the evidence before making the settlement decision. Small scope issues can affect coverage, causation, and customer trust.

**L.4.85 Q:** Can you assume the minimum roof payment percentage applies to every roof loss? Does the schedule matter only after you decide the roof needs replacement?

**L.4.86 A:** No. When the schedule applies, the minimum roof payment percentage is twenty-five (25) percent. Confirm that the claim facts and applicable terms support using the schedule before relying on that amount.

## L.6 — Knowledge Check

**L.6.1** **Q:** What does ACV mean in a roof claim? Explain it in practical claim terms.

**L.6.2** **A:** ACV is the value assigned to covered roof damage after applicable depreciation is considered. It is not automatically the same as the cost to install new materials.

**L.6.3** **Q:** Why does the ACV schedule matter? What claim decision can it support?

**L.6.4** **A:** The schedule can help you apply depreciation consistently to roof materials. It supports a documented valuation rather than an unsupported estimate.

**L.6.5** **Q:** Does an ACV payment mean the roof was not covered? What should you explain to the customer?

**L.6.6** **A:** No, an ACV valuation can still reflect covered damage. Explain that coverage and valuation are related but separate claim questions.

**L.6.7** **Q:** What should you identify before using an ACV schedule? What information affects the analysis?

**L.6.8** **A:** Identify the damaged roofing material and its observed condition. You also need reliable information about age, maintenance, and the cause of loss.

**L.6.9** **Q:** Can you use the schedule without inspecting the roof condition? Why is that risky?

**L.6.10** **A:** You should not treat the schedule as a substitute for claim investigation. Visible wear, prior repairs, and material condition can affect whether the scheduled result makes sense.

**L.6.11** **Q:** What is the difference between wear and storm damage? Why does the distinction matter?

**L.6.12** **A:** Wear develops through ordinary aging and exposure. Storm damage results from a covered event and should be evaluated based on physical evidence.

**L.6.13** **Q:** Can damaged and undamaged roof areas receive the same treatment? What should guide your decision?

**L.6.14** **A:** Review the condition of each relevant area rather than assuming all roof surfaces are alike. Your file should show why the claimed damage is connected to the reported event.

**L.6.15** **Q:** What does depreciation represent in an ACV evaluation? How should you describe it plainly?

**L.6.16** **A:** Depreciation reflects the loss of value associated with age, use, and condition. It is not a penalty for reporting a claim.

**L.6.17** **Q:** Should you apply depreciation because a roof looks old? What evidence should support it?

**L.6.18** **A:** Appearance alone may not establish the material’s condition or remaining usefulness. Support the valuation with inspection observations, reliable history, and claim documentation.

**L.6.19** **Q:** How should you handle a roof with prior repairs? What should the file show?

**L.6.20** **A:** Prior repairs may help explain existing conditions that are unrelated to the reported loss. Document where those repairs appear and how they affect your damage assessment.

**L.6.21** **Q:** What if the insured disagrees with the roof age used in the analysis? How should you respond?

**L.6.22** **A:** Ask for information that may clarify the roof’s installation history or material condition. Consider the information fairly and explain any change in your evaluation.

**L.6.23** **Q:** Can photographs support an ACV decision? What makes photographs useful?

**L.6.24** **A:** Photographs can show material type, condition, damage patterns, and prior repairs. They are most useful when they are clear, labeled, and connected to your observations.

**L.6.25** **Q:** What if photographs and the inspection notes do not match? What should you do?

**L.6.26** **A:** Resolve the inconsistency before relying on the valuation. Update the file with the reason for the correction and the evidence supporting it.

**L.6.27** **Q:** Does the ACV schedule decide whether damage was caused by wind? What is its proper role?

**L.6.28** **A:** No, the schedule is a valuation tool rather than a cause-of-loss tool. Establish the cause of damage through the investigation before applying a value.

**L.6.29** **Q:** How should you handle mixed damage on a roof? What must remain clear?

**L.6.30** **A:** Separate damage linked to the reported event from unrelated conditions where the evidence permits. Your estimate and explanation should make that distinction understandable.

**L.6.31** **Q:** What is a common mistake when applying ACV? How can you avoid it?

**L.6.32** **A:** A common mistake is treating a schedule result as automatic without reviewing the actual roof. Avoid that error by matching the schedule input to verified claim facts.

**L.6.33** **Q:** Should you explain the valuation to the insured? What should the explanation cover?

**L.6.34** **A:** Yes, explain the material facts used in the evaluation and the basis for the ACV result. Use clear language that distinguishes damage findings from valuation findings.

**L.6.35** **Q:** How does a deductible relate to an ACV payment? What should you avoid implying?

**L.6.36** **A:** A deductible may affect the amount payable after the covered loss is valued. Do not imply that the deductible determines whether damage is covered.

**L.6.37** **Q:** When should you consider the maximum windstorm deductible in seacoast territories? What claim fact triggers that review?

**L.6.38** **A:** Consider it when the reported loss involves windstorm conditions and the property location raises that issue. Keep the deductible review separate from your damage and valuation analysis.

**L.6.39** **Q:** How does the clean-up allowance percentage fit into a roof claim? Is it part of roof depreciation?

**L.6.40** **A:** Treat the clean-up allowance percentage as its own claim consideration. Do not blend it into the ACV analysis unless the claim terms direct that result.

**L.6.41** **Q:** What does the additional amount percentage change in your review? What should remain separate?

**L.6.42** **A:** The additional amount percentage may affect a claim calculation when it applies. Keep that issue distinct from your determination of roof damage and depreciation.

**L.6.43** **Q:** Why should you track the acknowledgment of claim deadline? How does it help the claim process?

**L.6.44** **A:** Tracking the acknowledgment of claim deadline helps you provide a timely response to the reported loss. It does not replace a careful investigation or clear communication.

**L.6.45** **Q:** What is the purpose of the duty to mitigate window? What should you communicate?

**L.6.46** **A:** The duty to mitigate window concerns reasonable steps to prevent additional damage after a loss. Explain the need to protect the property while preserving evidence of the damage.

**L.6.47** **Q:** Can emergency work affect the roof investigation? What should you request?

**L.6.48** **A:** Emergency work can change or conceal physical evidence. Request photographs, invoices, and a description of the work so the file explains what occurred.

**L.6.49** **Q:** What should an estimate match? Why is that important?

**L.6.50** **A:** The estimate should match the supported scope of covered damage. A clear match helps the insured understand the valuation and helps reviewers follow your reasoning.

**L.6.51** **Q:** What should you do when claim facts are incomplete? Can the schedule fill the gap?

**L.6.52** **A:** Continue gathering reliable information needed to evaluate the claim. The schedule cannot correct missing facts about damage, material, condition, or cause of loss.

**L.6.53** **Q:** What is the best final check before communicating an ACV result? What should be easy to follow?

**L.6.54** **A:** Confirm that your inspection findings, photographs, estimate, and valuation reasoning tell the same story. The file should clearly show how you moved from observed damage to the ACV conclusion.
