# Guidance Versus Contract Language







## L.1 — Learning Objectives

**L.1.1** You will learn to distinguish guidance from contract language. This distinction helps you explain coverage clearly without changing what the policy says.

**L.1.2** Guidance helps you understand a form, a claim issue, or an underwriting concern. Contract language creates the rights, duties, limits, conditions, and exclusions that apply.

**L.1.3** You should use guidance to support sound analysis. You should use the contract to determine the coverage position.

**L.1.4** A training explanation may use plain language, examples, and shortcuts. The policy may use defined terms and precise wording that lead to a different result.

**L.1.5** You will learn not to treat a simplified explanation as a promise of coverage. A helpful summary cannot broaden, restrict, or replace contract language.

**L.1.6** When you read a policy, pay attention to defined terms. A familiar word may have a special meaning when the contract defines it.

**L.1.7** You will learn to read relevant wording in context. A phrase may depend on nearby language, an exclusion, a condition, or an endorsement.

**L.1.8** Do not stop your review after finding language that appears favorable to the insured. Continue looking for language that changes, limits, or clarifies that result.

**L.1.9** You will learn that endorsements can alter the base policy wording. Read them carefully to understand which language applies to the risk or loss.

**L.1.10** Guidance can identify questions you should ask. It should not lead you to assume facts that are not supported by the application, file, or investigation.

**L.1.11** As an underwriter, you will use guidance to recognize exposures and request useful information. Your underwriting decision must remain consistent with the applicable contract and company authority.

**L.1.12** As an adjuster, you will use guidance to organize your investigation and explain your reasoning. Your coverage decision must follow the policy wording and the established facts.

**L.1.13** You will learn to separate facts from conclusions. Facts describe what happened, while conclusions explain how the contract responds to those facts.

**L.1.14** Avoid presenting guidance as if it were policy language. Say when you are providing a general explanation rather than quoting or applying the contract.

**L.1.15** You will learn to communicate uncertainty appropriately. If the available information does not support a clear answer, identify what you still need to review.

**L.1.16** Clear communication does not mean oversimplifying the issue. You can use plain language while preserving important qualifications from the contract.

**L.1.17** You should not rely on memory when the wording matters. Review the applicable policy language before giving a coverage explanation or documenting a decision.

**L.1.18** You will learn to document the basis for your analysis. A useful file note connects the relevant facts to the contract language you applied.

**L.1.19** Guidance may describe a common outcome. A common outcome is not necessarily the outcome for the particular policy, insured, or loss.

**L.1.20** You will learn to recognize when a question requires further review. Escalate issues that involve unclear wording, conflicting provisions, unusual facts, or authority concerns.

**L.1.21** Treat examples as learning tools rather than binding interpretations. Change the facts or wording, and the result may change as well.

**L.1.22** By the end of this material, you should be able to use guidance confidently without confusing it with the contract. You should also be able to explain why the policy language remains the basis for the final coverage decision.

## L.2 — Key Concepts

**L.2.1** **Q:** What is guidance?

**L.2.2** **A:** Guidance helps you understand how to approach a coverage question. It supports consistent thinking, but it does not create coverage.

**L.2.3** **Q:** What is contract language?

**L.2.4** **A:** Contract language states the agreement that controls the coverage decision. You must rely on that language when you determine whether coverage applies.

**L.2.5** **Q:** Why does the difference matter?

**L.2.6** **A:** Guidance may simplify a difficult issue for training purposes. Contract language may contain conditions, exceptions, or details that the simplified explanation does not show.

**L.2.7** **Q:** Can guidance change the contract language?

**L.2.8** **A:** No. Guidance cannot add coverage, remove coverage, or revise an obligation. The contract language controls even when guidance sounds broader or narrower.

**L.2.9** **Q:** Can you use guidance during a coverage review?

**L.2.10** **A:** Yes. Use guidance to identify issues, organize facts, and recognize language that may matter. Then confirm your conclusion against the contract language.

**L.2.11** **Q:** What should you do when guidance and contract language seem different?

**L.2.12** **A:** Follow the contract language. Treat the difference as a signal that you should slow down and review the wording carefully.

**L.2.13** **Q:** Is guidance always complete?

**L.2.14** **A:** No. Guidance often focuses on common situations and practical habits. A particular loss may involve facts or language that the guidance does not address.

**L.2.15** **Q:** Is contract language always easy to read?

**L.2.16** **A:** No. Contract language can be detailed and technical. Your job is to read it closely without replacing it with a shortcut.

**L.2.17** **Q:** What is a useful way to start a coverage analysis?

**L.2.18** **A:** Start with the reported facts and identify the coverage question. Then locate the contract language that addresses that question.

**L.2.19** **Q:** Should you begin with a conclusion from guidance?

**L.2.20** **A:** No. Guidance may suggest issues to investigate, but it should not become your conclusion. Build the conclusion from the facts and contract language.

**L.2.21** **Q:** How should you use a training summary?

**L.2.22** **A:** Use a training summary to understand the general purpose of coverage language. Do not treat the summary as a replacement for the language itself.

**L.2.23** **Q:** What happens when a simplified explanation leaves out an exception?

**L.2.24** **A:** The exception may still affect the outcome. You must consider all relevant contract language, including language that limits or restores coverage.

**L.2.25** **Q:** Can a familiar claim pattern replace a language review?

**L.2.26** **A:** No. Familiar facts can create assumptions that do not fit the actual wording. Review the language rather than relying on memory.

**L.2.27** **Q:** Why can assumptions cause problems?

**L.2.28** **A:** Assumptions may cause you to overlook an important fact or condition. They can also lead you to apply a general rule where the contract language requires a different result.

**L.2.29** **Q:** What should you do if a customer quotes guidance?

**L.2.30** **A:** Listen to the concern and identify the coverage issue being raised. Explain that the coverage decision must be based on the applicable contract language and facts.

**L.2.31** **Q:** Should you describe guidance as a promise?

**L.2.32** **A:** No. Guidance is an educational tool, not a promise of payment or denial. Use careful language when discussing coverage with others.

**L.2.33** **Q:** What is the risk of using broad statements?

**L.2.34** **A:** Broad statements can sound more certain than the contract language allows. They may also overlook facts that could change the analysis.

**L.2.35** **Q:** How can you make your explanation more accurate?

**L.2.36** **A:** Tie your explanation to the relevant facts and contract language. Avoid describing a general rule as though it answers every situation.

**L.2.37** **Q:** What should you do when the facts are unclear?

**L.2.38** **A:** Identify what information is missing and why it matters. Do not force a conclusion from guidance when the facts remain incomplete.

**L.2.39** **Q:** Can guidance help you identify missing facts?

**L.2.40** **A:** Yes. Guidance can help you recognize the types of facts that often affect a coverage analysis. You still need to connect those facts to the contract language.

**L.2.41** **Q:** What is the role of definitions?

**L.2.42** **A:** Definitions can give important words a meaning that differs from everyday use. Read defined terms carefully before relying on a common understanding.

**L.2.43** **Q:** Should you rely on ordinary meanings when a term is defined?

**L.2.44** **A:** No. The defined meaning controls within the contract language. Guidance may alert you to defined terms, but it cannot replace their stated meaning.

**L.2.45** **Q:** What is the role of exclusions?

**L.2.46** **A:** Exclusions may limit coverage that otherwise appears available. Review them as part of the full analysis rather than treating coverage language as the end of the review.

**L.2.47** **Q:** What is the role of conditions?

**L.2.48** **A:** Conditions may affect duties, obligations, or the handling of a claim. Guidance can describe common concerns, but the contract language states the actual requirement.

**L.2.49** **Q:** What is the role of exceptions?

**L.2.50** **A:** Exceptions can affect how a limitation applies. Read the surrounding language carefully so you do not stop at an incomplete answer.

**L.2.51** **Q:** Why should you read related language together?

**L.2.52** **A:** A sentence may not make sense when separated from its context. Related language can explain scope, limits, conditions, or exceptions.

**L.2.53** **Q:** Can a heading decide coverage?

**L.2.54** **A:** No. A heading can help you find relevant language, but it does not replace the operative wording. Read the actual language beneath the heading.

**L.2.55** **Q:** Can an example decide coverage?

**L.2.56** **A:** No. An example illustrates a possible situation and may omit important facts. Compare the actual claim facts with the contract language.

**L.2.57** **Q:** Why might an example be incomplete?

**L.2.58** **A:** Examples are designed to teach a point without covering every variation. A real claim may involve additional facts that change the analysis.

**L.2.59** **Q:** What should you do when a claim resembles a training example?

**L.2.60** **A:** Use the resemblance as a prompt to investigate the issue. Do not assume that the example determines the result.

**L.2.61** **Q:** Can past handling determine a current decision?

**L.2.62** **A:** No. Past handling may offer context, but it does not replace review of the current facts and contract language. Each claim requires its own analysis.

**L.2.63** **Q:** Why should you avoid copying prior explanations?

**L.2.64** **A:** A prior explanation may be tied to different facts or language. Reuse the reasoning approach, not an unsupported conclusion.

**L.2.65** **Q:** What makes guidance useful?

**L.2.66** **A:** Guidance helps you ask better questions and recognize common coverage issues. It is most useful when you use it alongside careful language review.

**L.2.67** **Q:** What makes guidance risky?

**L.2.68** **A:** Guidance becomes risky when you treat it as controlling language. It can also cause problems when you overlook facts that do not fit the simplified explanation.

**L.2.69** **Q:** How should you handle a close coverage question?

**L.2.70** **A:** Read the relevant contract language carefully and compare it with the known facts. Escalate the question through the appropriate process when additional review is needed.

**L.2.71** **Q:** Should you guess when the wording is unclear?

**L.2.72** **A:** No. Identify the uncertainty and seek appropriate support. A guessed answer can create an inaccurate coverage position.

**L.2.73** **Q:** What should your claim notes show?

**L.2.74** **A:** Your notes should show the relevant facts, the issue considered, and the reasoning used. They should make clear that the analysis rests on contract language rather than guidance alone.

**L.2.75** **Q:** Why is clear documentation important?

**L.2.76** **A:** Clear documentation helps others understand how you reached the coverage position. It also helps you identify gaps in facts or reasoning.

**L.2.77** **Q:** Should claim notes quote broad training statements?

**L.2.78** **A:** Avoid relying on broad training statements as the basis for a decision. Document the relevant language and explain how it applies to the facts.

**L.2.79** **Q:** How should you explain a denial?

**L.2.80** **A:** Explain the decision accurately and respectfully using the relevant contract language. Do not rely on a simplified training phrase when the wording requires a more careful explanation.

**L.2.81** **Q:** How should you explain available coverage?

**L.2.82** **A:** Describe the coverage position based on the facts and contract language. Avoid making broader promises than the language supports.

**L.2.83** **Q:** What should you do when guidance uses everyday language?

**L.2.84** **A:** Treat everyday language as a learning aid. Check whether the contract language uses a defined or more limited meaning.

**L.2.85** **Q:** Can guidance describe a typical result?

**L.2.86** **A:** Yes. A typical result can help you understand common handling patterns. It does not decide an unusual claim or override the controlling language.

**L.2.87** **Q:** Why do unusual facts matter?

**L.2.88** **A:** Unusual facts may bring different language into the analysis. They can also prevent a familiar rule from applying as expected.

**L.2.89** **Q:** What should you do when facts change during the investigation?

**L.2.90** **A:** Revisit the coverage analysis using the updated information. Do not continue using an earlier conclusion if the factual basis has changed.

**L.2.91** **Q:** Can a strong initial impression be wrong?

**L.2.92** **A:** Yes. An initial impression may be based on incomplete facts or a broad understanding of coverage. Let the full review guide the final position.

**L.2.93** **Q:** How can you avoid premature conclusions?

**L.2.94** **A:** Separate what you know from what you still need to confirm. Review the contract language before communicating a firm coverage position.

**L.2.95** **Q:** What should you do when guidance seems more generous than the wording?

**L.2.96** **A:** Follow the wording. Guidance may have simplified the issue or described a different factual setting.

**L.2.97** **Q:** What should you do when guidance seems more restrictive than the wording?

**L.2.98** **A:** Follow the wording. Do not deny coverage based on a simplified training statement that is narrower than the applicable language.

**L.2.99** **Q:** Why is context important?

**L.2.100** **A:** Context helps you understand how language works within the full agreement. Reading isolated phrases can lead to incomplete or inaccurate conclusions.

**L.2.101** **Q:** Can a keyword search complete the analysis?

**L.2.102** **A:** No. A keyword search can help you find language, but it cannot show how related wording affects the issue. Read the surrounding provisions.

**L.2.103** **Q:** What is a good question to ask while reading guidance?

**L.2.104** **A:** Ask what issue the guidance is trying to help you recognize. Then ask what contract language and facts control that issue.

**L.2.105** **Q:** What is a good question to ask while reading contract language?

**L.2.106** **A:** Ask what the language requires, permits, limits, or excludes. Then determine whether the claim facts satisfy that language.

**L.2.107** **Q:** Should you separate facts from interpretations?

**L.2.108** **A:** Yes. Facts describe what happened, while interpretations explain what the facts may mean. Keeping them separate helps you test your reasoning.

**L.2.109** **Q:** Why should you identify the exact coverage question?

**L.2.110** **A:** A clear question keeps the review focused on the relevant language and facts. It also reduces the chance that a general discussion will replace a specific analysis.

**L.2.111** **Q:** What is the main rule to remember?

**L.2.112** **A:** Use guidance to learn and to organize your review. Use contract language and claim facts to determine the coverage position.

## L.3 — Worked Examples

**L.3.1 Q:** What should you do when training guidance summarizes coverage? Should you treat the summary as the coverage promise?

**L.3.2 A:** Use the guidance to identify the issue you need to review. Read the contract language before you state a coverage position.

**L.3.3 Q:** A guide says a loss is “usually covered.” Is that enough to approve the claim? Does “usually” replace the form wording?

**L.3.4 A:** No. “Usually” is a training shortcut that may omit conditions, exclusions, or endorsements. Confirm the actual coverage grant and all applicable limitations.

**L.3.5 Q:** A claimant relies on a brochure description of coverage. How should you respond? What source controls the claim decision?

**L.3.6 A:** Acknowledge the brochure and review it for context. Explain that the contract language controls the coverage decision.

**L.3.7 Q:** Can you deny a claim based only on an internal job aid? What should you review before communicating a denial?

**L.3.8 A:** An internal job aid can help you find the relevant issue. Your decision must be supported by the policy language and the documented facts.

**L.3.9 Q:** A quick-reference chart uses plain language that seems broader than the endorsement. Which wording should you follow? How should you document the difference?

**L.3.10 A:** Follow the endorsement because it changes the contract terms. Note the chart’s simplified wording only as a training observation.

**L.3.11 Q:** What if guidance says an item is personal property, but the form defines it differently? Can you rely on the label used in guidance?

**L.3.12 A:** No. Apply the definition found in the contract language. Guidance labels are useful only when they match the actual form.

**L.3.13 Q:** A customer says an agent made a verbal promise. Does that statement automatically create coverage? What should you investigate?

**L.3.14 A:** Do not assume that a verbal statement changes the policy. Review the issued documents, endorsements, application materials, and claim facts.

**L.3.15 Q:** A training example reaches a favorable outcome. Must every similar claim receive the same result? What may make the result different?

**L.3.16 A:** No. Training examples simplify facts to teach a point. Differences in wording, endorsements, property, cause of loss, or evidence may change the result.

**L.3.17 Q:** What is the safest way to use a coverage checklist? Can a completed checklist replace review of the form?

**L.3.18 A:** Use the checklist to make sure you investigate relevant issues. It does not replace reading the operative policy provisions.

**L.3.19 Q:** A guide calls an exclusion “common.” Does that mean it applies to every policy? What should you verify?

**L.3.20 A:** No. An exclusion applies only if it is part of the issued contract. Verify the policy edition, endorsements, and effective wording.

**L.3.21 Q:** How should you handle a definition that appears in more than one part of the policy? Can you use only the definition that seems easiest?

**L.3.22 A:** Read each relevant provision in context. The location and wording of a definition can affect how it applies.

**L.3.23 Q:** A note in guidance says to “check exclusions.” What is your next step? Is it enough to identify an exclusion by name?

**L.3.24 A:** Read the full exclusion and any exceptions that follow it. Then compare its requirements to the established facts.

**L.3.25 Q:** The AOP deductible floor appears in a training discussion. Does that discussion establish the deductible for a particular loss? What should you inspect?

**L.3.26 A:** No. Review the declarations, deductible wording, and applicable endorsements. The claim file should show why the selected deductible applies.

**L.3.27 Q:** A claimant asks whether the replacement cost threshold percentage affects payment. Can you answer from memory? What should you do instead?

**L.3.28 A:** Review the valuation provision and any related conditions. Explain the result only after confirming the applicable contract wording and facts.

**L.3.29 Q:** A guide mentions the special limit on theft of jewelry, watches and precious stones. Does that mention decide the amount payable? What else may matter?

**L.3.30 A:** No. Review the applicable limit language, coverage grant, and any scheduled property endorsement. The facts of ownership, theft, and valuation may also matter.

**L.3.31 Q:** A claim involves water entering through a drain. Is the sewer backup limit automatically the controlling provision? What should you determine first?

**L.3.32 A:** Determine the source and path of the water based on reliable evidence. Then apply the policy provisions that match those facts.

**L.3.33 Q:** A storm loss raises a question about the hurricane deductible floor. Should you assume that deductible applies because the event involved wind? What should you verify?

**L.3.34 A:** No. Review the event definition, deductible terms, endorsements, and loss facts. Document the basis for the deductible decision.

**L.3.35 Q:** A guide says “wear and tear is not covered.” Is that statement complete? What might the form require you to consider?

**L.3.36 A:** The statement may be incomplete because resulting damage, exceptions, and concurrent causes can matter. Read the exclusion and the surrounding coverage language.

**L.3.37 Q:** A claim note describes damage as sudden. Does that word settle coverage? How should you evaluate it?

**L.3.38 A:** Treat the description as an allegation that needs support. Compare the reported timing, inspection findings, and policy requirements.

**L.3.39 Q:** A training handout uses the term “accidental.” Should you assume the policy uses that term the same way? What is the better approach?

**L.3.40 A:** Check whether the contract defines the term or uses different language. Apply the policy wording rather than the handout’s shorthand.

**L.3.41 Q:** An adjuster says a loss is excluded because it developed over time. Is that conclusion complete without further analysis? What facts should be gathered?

**L.3.42 A:** Gather facts about the cause, duration, damage pattern, and any triggering event. Then test those facts against the actual exclusions and exceptions.

**L.3.43 Q:** A guide describes a requirement as a “condition of coverage.” Can you rely on that label alone? What must you read?

**L.3.44 A:** Read the condition itself and identify what it requires. Also review the consequences stated in the policy if the condition is not met.

**L.3.45 Q:** A claimant submitted some documents after a loss. How do you decide whether a proof of loss is needed? Should you assume every document serves the same purpose?

**L.3.46 A:** Review the duties after loss and any request sent to the claimant. Different documents may satisfy different policy requirements.

**L.3.47 Q:** When must a signed, sworn proof of loss be submitted after it is properly requested? How should you explain the requirement?

**L.3.48 A:** The insured must submit it within sixty (60) days. State the requirement clearly and preserve the request and response in the claim file.

**L.3.49 Q:** A claimant gives an oral account of the damage. Does that automatically satisfy every post-loss duty? What should you check?

**L.3.50 A:** No. Review the duties after loss and the information requested. Document which requirements have been met and which remain outstanding.

**L.3.51 Q:** A guide says to request repair records. Must you request them in every claim? What should drive the request?

**L.3.52 A:** Request records that are relevant to the coverage issue or amount of loss. Tailor the request to the facts rather than using a routine demand.

**L.3.53 Q:** Can you use a reservation of rights letter as a final coverage decision? What is the purpose of that letter?

**L.3.54 A:** A reservation of rights preserves the insurer’s ability to investigate while identifying potential issues. It is not a substitute for a completed coverage analysis.

**L.3.55 Q:** A denial template contains language that fits most claims. Can you send it without changes? What should the final letter reflect?

**L.3.56 A:** No. The letter should accurately identify the relevant facts and policy provisions. Remove wording that does not apply to the claim.

**L.3.57 Q:** A coverage letter quotes a policy provision. Is quoting the provision enough? What else should the letter do?

**L.3.58 A:** Explain how the provision applies to the known facts. Use clear language that does not expand or narrow the contract.

**L.3.59 Q:** A claimant disagrees with your interpretation of a term. Should you argue from training materials? What should you rely on?

**L.3.60 A:** Rely on the policy language and the documented investigation. Escalate interpretation questions through the appropriate internal process.

**L.3.61 Q:** An endorsement appears to conflict with the base policy. Which source should you review carefully? Why is that important?

**L.3.62 A:** Review both provisions and the endorsement’s modifying language. An endorsement may add, remove, or revise coverage terms.

**L.3.63 Q:** A policy includes manuscript wording. Can a generic guide explain it fully? What should you do with unusual language?

**L.3.64 A:** Generic guidance may not address manuscript wording. Read the manuscript provision closely and seek technical support when needed.

**L.3.65 Q:** A claim file contains an old policy copy. Can you assume it is the governing policy? What must be confirmed?

**L.3.66 A:** Confirm the policy in force for the reported loss. Verify the declarations, forms, endorsements, and applicable version.

**L.3.67 Q:** A document is missing from the file. Should you fill the gap with a standard assumption? What is the better response?

**L.3.68 A:** Do not assume the missing document contains standard wording. Obtain the correct document or identify the limitation in your analysis.

**L.3.69 Q:** A guide uses an example involving a named peril. Can you apply it to an open-peril form without review? What may differ?

**L.3.70 A:** No. The burden of proof and coverage structure may differ. Start with the coverage grant in the issued form.

**L.3.71 Q:** A guide says an item is “scheduled.” Does that mean it is covered for every type of loss? What should you examine?

**L.3.72 A:** Review the schedule, endorsement terms, and applicable exclusions. Scheduling can change valuation or coverage, but its effect depends on the wording.

**L.3.73 Q:** A claimant has receipts for damaged property. Do receipts alone establish payment? What else may be relevant?

**L.3.74 A:** Receipts can support ownership and value, but they do not establish coverage by themselves. Review the cause of loss, property status, and valuation terms.

**L.3.75 Q:** An inspection report uses technical language. Can you convert that language directly into a coverage conclusion? What should you separate?

**L.3.76 A:** Separate the expert’s factual observations from the policy interpretation. Use the report to establish facts, then apply the contract language.

**L.3.77 Q:** A contractor says damage resulted from a covered event. Does that opinion control the claim? How should you evaluate it?

**L.3.78 A:** Consider the opinion along with other available evidence. The policy application remains the insurer’s responsibility.

**L.3.79 Q:** A claimant describes a prior repair. Does that fact automatically defeat coverage? What should you investigate?

**L.3.80 A:** Determine what was repaired, why it was repaired, and whether it relates to the claimed damage. Apply the relevant policy language after the facts are developed.

**L.3.81 Q:** A guide tells you to look for maintenance issues. Does poor maintenance always end the analysis? What may still require review?

**L.3.82 A:** No. You must still identify the cause of loss and evaluate all relevant provisions. An exclusion may include wording that changes the outcome.

**L.3.83 Q:** A loss has more than one possible cause. Can you choose the most obvious cause and stop? What should your file show?

**L.3.84 A:** Investigate each material cause supported by the evidence. Your file should explain how the policy responds to the combined facts.

**L.3.85 Q:** A training summary says “damage from water” without more detail. Is that enough to determine coverage? What distinctions may matter?

**L.3.86 A:** No. The source, movement, location, and cause of water can be important. Match those facts to the policy’s specific wording.

**L.3.87 Q:** A claimant asks for a simple answer before the investigation is complete. Can you promise coverage based on an initial report? What can you say?

**L.3.88 A:** Avoid making a coverage promise before the facts and policy are reviewed. Explain that the claim is being evaluated under the applicable contract terms.

**L.3.89 Q:** Can you tell a claimant that guidance requires a particular result? Why is that risky?

**L.3.90 A:** That wording is risky because guidance is not the insurance contract. Explain the decision using the policy provisions that apply.

**L.3.91 Q:** A supervisor gives you an informal interpretation. Does that eliminate the need to read the form? How should you use the direction?

**L.3.92 A:** Use the direction as support for your review, not as a replacement for it. Make sure the file reflects the operative wording and facts.

**L.3.93 Q:** A claim involves property used for business activity. Can a household-property example decide the issue? What should you review?

**L.3.94 A:** Review the policy definitions, property coverage terms, and relevant exclusions. The actual use of the property may affect coverage.

**L.3.95 Q:** A claimant says an item belonged to a family member. Does that resolve whether the item is insured property? What should you confirm?

**L.3.96 A:** Confirm the relationship, residency, ownership, and policy definitions. Do not rely only on a general description of the item.

**L.3.97 Q:** A guide says a vacancy issue may exist. Should you deny the claim immediately? What comes before the decision?

**L.3.98 A:** Investigate the property’s use, occupancy, condition, and relevant time frame. Then apply the vacancy wording exactly as written.

**L.3.99 Q:** A loss occurred away from the insured location. Can you assume there is no coverage? What should you check?

**L.3.100 A:** Review the property coverage grant and any off-premises provisions. Endorsements and the type of property may affect the analysis.

**L.3.101 Q:** A claimant moved property before inspection. Does that prevent all recovery? What should you determine?

**L.3.102 A:** Determine why the property was moved and whether the move affected the investigation. Review the duties after loss and any prejudice issues under the policy.

**L.3.103 Q:** A claim involves emergency work. Does the word “emergency” establish reimbursement? What should you evaluate?

**L.3.104 A:** Evaluate the nature of the work, its purpose, and the applicable policy provisions. Necessary mitigation and covered repair are not always the same issue.

**L.3.105 Q:** A guide recommends prompt inspection. Does a delayed inspection automatically determine coverage? What should you document?

**L.3.106 A:** Document the reason for the delay and the effect on available evidence. Apply the policy conditions based on the actual circumstances.

**L.3.107 Q:** A claimant cannot produce the damaged item. Must you reject the claim without further review? What evidence may still be useful?

**L.3.108 A:** Consider photographs, receipts, witness statements, records, and inspection findings. Evaluate the evidence fairly under the policy requirements.

**L.3.109 Q:** A policy term seems unclear when read alone. Should you select the interpretation that appears most convenient? What should you read?

**L.3.110 A:** Read the term in the context of the full policy. Related definitions, conditions, and endorsements may clarify its meaning.

**L.3.111 Q:** A guide includes a broad statement about exclusions. Can an exception restore coverage? What must you do?

**L.3.112 A:** Yes, an exception can affect the exclusion’s operation. Read the entire provision, including all exceptions and related language.

**L.3.113 Q:** A claimant asks whether a prior claim affects the current loss. Can you answer from a general rule? What facts matter?

**L.3.114 A:** Review the prior claim materials and the current damage evidence. The connection between the losses must be established, not assumed.

**L.3.115 Q:** A repair estimate includes upgrades. Should you treat every line as covered repair cost? What should you separate?

**L.3.116 A:** Separate repair of covered damage from betterment, unrelated work, and unsupported items. Then apply the valuation provisions to the supported loss.

**L.3.117 Q:** A guide describes replacement cost in plain language. Is that enough to calculate payment? What should you review?

**L.3.118 A:** Review the valuation terms, conditions, and applicable endorsements. The policy may distinguish among several measures of loss.

**L.3.119 Q:** A claimant requests payment based on a contractor estimate. Does the estimate set the value of the loss? What role does it serve?

**L.3.120 A:** An estimate is evidence that should be evaluated with other information. Compare its scope, pricing, and assumptions to the covered damage.

**L.3.121 Q:** A note says “refer to legal” for difficult wording. Should you stop investigating while waiting? What work can continue?

**L.3.122 A:** Continue gathering facts and preserving evidence relevant to the coverage question. Avoid final conclusions until the interpretation issue is resolved.

**L.3.123 Q:** A claimant asks why guidance and the policy seem different. How can you answer clearly? What should you avoid saying?

**L.3.124 A:** Explain that guidance is designed to simplify common issues, while the policy contains the binding terms. Avoid suggesting that the guide changes the contract.

**L.3.125 Q:** What should a strong claim note say when guidance helped identify an issue? Should the note cite guidance as the basis for the decision?

**L.3.126 A:** State that the issue was reviewed and identify the policy language applied. The decision rationale should rest on facts and contract terms.

**L.3.127 Q:** A training example uses a favorable fact pattern. Can you assume missing facts are favorable too? How should you handle uncertainty?

**L.3.128 A:** No. Identify the missing facts and seek reliable evidence. If uncertainty remains, follow the applicable claim-handling process.

**L.3.129 Q:** A form is difficult to read because of an unclear scan. Can you rely on a guessed word? What should you do instead?

**L.3.130 A:** Do not base a coverage decision on uncertain wording. Obtain a legible copy or another reliable source for the issued language.

**L.3.131 Q:** What is the main lesson when guidance and contract language point in different directions? How should you protect the quality of your decision?

**L.3.132 A:** The contract language controls, and guidance helps you ask better questions. Build your decision from the issued policy, applicable endorsements, and well-documented facts.

## L.4 — Common Mistakes

**L.4.1 Q:** Can you treat underwriting guidance as if it changes coverage?

**L.4.2 A:** No. Guidance helps you recognize issues and make consistent decisions, but it does not alter the contract. You must separate a helpful summary from the language that controls coverage.

**L.4.3 Q:** What is a common mistake when reading a training guide?

**L.4.4 A:** A common mistake is assuming every statement applies to every risk. Check whether the statement describes an underwriting preference, a claim-handling practice, or a contract requirement.

**L.4.5 Q:** Can a risk be outside appetite even when coverage language would otherwise respond?

**L.4.6 A:** Yes. Appetite is a business decision about whether to offer or continue coverage. Coverage language addresses what applies after a policy is issued.

**L.4.7 Q:** How should you handle roof age guidance?

**L.4.8 A:** Treat it as an underwriting screen unless the contract says otherwise. A roof age at or above twenty-five (25) years is outside appetite.

**L.4.9 Q:** Does an underwriting decline mean a loss would never have been covered?

**L.4.10 A:** No. A decline decision and a coverage decision answer different questions. Do not use underwriting guidance to rewrite the policy after a loss.

**L.4.11 Q:** Can you deny a claim because the risk would not be acceptable today?

**L.4.12 A:** No. Start with the policy language, endorsements, facts, and applicable claim requirements. Current appetite does not decide coverage under an issued policy.

**L.4.13 Q:** Is a checklist the same as a policy condition?

**L.4.14 A:** No. A checklist may help you gather facts, but it is not automatically a contractual duty. Look for the actual condition before telling an insured that compliance is required.

**L.4.15 Q:** What happens when guidance uses plain language but the contract uses technical language?

**L.4.16 A:** Use the guidance to understand the general issue, then read the contract language carefully. The technical wording may be narrower or broader than the training summary.

**L.4.17 Q:** Can you rely on a coverage label alone?

**L.4.18 A:** No. A label can be useful shorthand, but it does not explain all limits, exclusions, conditions, or definitions. Read the language connected to the label before making a decision.

**L.4.19 Q:** Why is it risky to use a memory aid as a coverage rule?

**L.4.20 A:** Memory aids leave out exceptions and details. They can help you identify what to review, but they should not replace review of the actual wording.

**L.4.21 Q:** Should you promise coverage while you are still reviewing the policy?

**L.4.22 A:** No. Explain that you are reviewing the facts and applicable policy language. A careful answer is better than a quick answer that overstates what the contract provides.

**L.4.23 Q:** Can an internal note create coverage?

**L.4.24 A:** No. An internal note may document a decision or explain handling direction. It does not replace the policy, endorsement, or other binding contract language.

**L.4.25 Q:** What is the mistake in saying that a loss is “always covered”?

**L.4.26 A:** That statement ignores the facts and the policy wording. Coverage may depend on cause of loss, property involved, exclusions, conditions, and endorsements.

**L.4.27 Q:** What is the mistake in saying that a loss is “never covered”?

**L.4.28 A:** That statement can ignore exceptions, additional coverage provisions, and endorsements. Use careful wording until you have reviewed the relevant contract terms.

**L.4.29 Q:** Can a training example decide a real claim?

**L.4.30 A:** No. A training example shows a reasoning method, not a final answer for every file. Your file may contain different facts, wording, or endorsements.

**L.4.31 Q:** How should you use examples that simplify difficult rules?

**L.4.32 A:** Use them to spot questions that need closer review. Do not assume the simplified outcome controls when the policy language says something different.

**L.4.33 Q:** Is a summary of an exclusion enough to deny a claim?

**L.4.34 A:** No. Read the exclusion itself and determine whether it applies to the reported facts. Also review any exceptions that may restore coverage.

**L.4.35 Q:** Can you assume an endorsement only adds coverage?

**L.4.36 A:** No. An endorsement can add, remove, or change coverage. Read it with the base policy rather than treating it as a separate summary.

**L.4.37 Q:** What is a common mistake with defined terms?

**L.4.38 A:** A common mistake is giving a word its everyday meaning instead of its policy meaning. If the policy defines a term, use that definition in your analysis.

**L.4.39 Q:** Should you assume similar wording has the same effect in every policy?

**L.4.40 A:** No. Small wording differences can matter. Review the language in the policy you are handling rather than relying on a similar file.

**L.4.41 Q:** Can marketing language settle a coverage question?

**L.4.42 A:** No. Marketing language may describe a product generally, but it is not a substitute for the contract. Base your decision on the policy language and the facts.

**L.4.43 Q:** What should you do when guidance and policy language appear inconsistent?

**L.4.44 A:** Follow the policy language for the coverage decision. Then flag the guidance issue so the training material can be clarified for future use.

**L.4.45 Q:** Can you use a policy declaration as the complete coverage answer?

**L.4.46 A:** No. The declaration identifies important information, but it may not show all applicable terms. Review the policy form and endorsements as part of the analysis.

**L.4.47 Q:** Is an underwriting question automatically a claim condition?

**L.4.48 A:** No. Underwriting questions help evaluate the risk before coverage is issued or renewed. Claim conditions come from the contract and must be handled as written.

**L.4.49 Q:** Can a missing underwriting detail automatically void coverage?

**L.4.50 A:** No. Do not jump from an incomplete file to a coverage conclusion. Determine what the contract says and whether the facts support the issue being considered.

**L.4.51 Q:** What is the mistake in treating all property concerns as maintenance exclusions?

**L.4.52 A:** Maintenance concerns may affect underwriting, but they do not automatically resolve a claim. Review the cause of loss and the specific policy provisions that apply.

**L.4.53 Q:** Can you treat risk-control advice as a contract obligation?

**L.4.54 A:** No. Risk-control advice may be valuable, but it is not necessarily mandatory. Confirm whether the policy actually requires the action before relying on it.

**L.4.55 Q:** How should you discuss the sworn proof deadline with an insured?

**L.4.56 A:** Explain the requirement carefully and use the policy language as your guide. Do not rely on a training shortcut when communicating a contractual obligation.

**L.4.57 Q:** Why should you avoid paraphrasing a legal condition too loosely?

**L.4.58 A:** Loose paraphrasing can change the meaning of the condition. When the wording matters, quote or closely track the applicable contract language.

**L.4.59 Q:** Can you tell an insured that any late action defeats a claim?

**L.4.60 A:** No. The effect of timing depends on the policy language, the facts, and applicable handling requirements. Review the issue before making a definite statement.

**L.4.61 Q:** How should you handle the years within which an action against the insurer must be brought?

**L.4.62 A:** Treat that language as a contract issue requiring careful review. Do not replace the policy wording with a general statement about legal rights.

**L.4.63 Q:** Is a renewal decision the same as a coverage determination?

**L.4.64 A:** No. Renewal decisions concern the insurer’s willingness to continue the risk. Coverage determinations concern obligations under an issued policy.

**L.4.65 Q:** What should you remember about the nonrenewal notice period?

**L.4.66 A:** Treat it as a requirement that must be confirmed from the applicable policy and process rules. Do not rely on a remembered rule when notice handling is involved.

**L.4.67 Q:** Can you assume a special coverage has no restrictions because it appears in a summary?

**L.4.68 A:** No. Special coverages often have their own conditions, exclusions, and definitions. Read the complete provision before explaining how it works.

**L.4.69 Q:** How should you handle assessment coverage limit questions?

**L.4.70 A:** Review the applicable coverage language and any endorsement that changes it. Avoid stating a limit based only on a training summary or prior file.

**L.4.71 Q:** Can you assume cleanup costs are always handled under property coverage?

**L.4.72 A:** No. Cleanup issues can involve separate provisions, exclusions, and definitions. Identify the cause and review the language that addresses the reported condition.

**L.4.73 Q:** What should you do with a microbial remediation limit question?

**L.4.74 A:** Check the policy language that applies to the reported facts. Do not describe the coverage as broad or narrow until you have reviewed the relevant terms.

**L.4.75 Q:** Is ordinance or law coverage as a percentage of Coverage A a complete explanation of that coverage?

**L.4.76 A:** No. That description may not explain triggers, restrictions, or applicable conditions. Read the full provision and any related endorsement before responding.

**L.4.77 Q:** Can you infer coverage from the fact that damage is visible?

**L.4.78 A:** No. Visible damage does not by itself establish a covered cause of loss. Determine what happened and compare the facts with the policy language.

**L.4.79 Q:** Can you infer an excluded cause from the condition of the property?

**L.4.80 A:** No. Property condition may raise questions, but it is not the complete answer. Investigate the loss facts before applying an exclusion.

**L.4.81 Q:** What is the best way to correct an overbroad statement to an insured?

**L.4.82 A:** Acknowledge that the earlier statement was incomplete and explain that you are reviewing the policy language. Then provide a clear answer supported by the contract and the facts.

**L.4.83 Q:** How can you avoid confusing guidance with contract language?

**L.4.84 A:** Use guidance to identify issues, organize your review, and ask better questions. Use the policy and endorsements to make the coverage decision.

**L.4.85 Q:** What is the safest final check before you communicate a decision?

**L.4.86 A:** Confirm that your explanation matches the applicable policy wording and the established facts. If your explanation depends only on a summary, pause and review the contract.

## L.6 — Knowledge Check

**L.6.1** **Q:** What is the main purpose of guidance material?  
Guidance helps you understand how to approach a coverage question and identify facts that may matter.

**L.6.2** **A:** Guidance is a learning tool, not the agreement with the customer.  
Use it to support your analysis, then confirm the applicable contract language.

**L.6.3** **Q:** Why can guidance and contract language sound different?  
Guidance often uses plain language so you can recognize common issues quickly.

**L.6.4** **A:** Contract language must address coverage, conditions, exclusions, and definitions precisely.  
That precision can make the wording less conversational than training material.

**L.6.5** **Q:** If guidance gives a simple rule, can you apply it without checking the policy?  
No, because the policy wording controls the coverage decision.

**L.6.6** **A:** The simple rule may help you spot the issue, but it may not capture every condition.  
Read the relevant wording before you communicate a decision.

**L.6.7** **Q:** What should you do when guidance appears broader than the policy wording?  
Treat the policy wording as controlling and avoid extending coverage through explanation.

**L.6.8** **A:** Guidance may describe a typical outcome rather than every possible outcome.  
Your file should show how the actual facts connect to the contract terms.

**L.6.9** **Q:** What should you do when guidance appears narrower than the policy wording?  
Do not use the guidance to restrict coverage that the contract provides.

**L.6.10** **A:** Review the wording carefully and apply it to the facts presented.  
If the issue remains unclear, seek the appropriate internal support.

**L.6.11** **Q:** Can an example in training material create coverage?  
No, an example cannot change the contract or add a promise to the policy.

**L.6.12** **A:** Examples are designed to teach reasoning patterns, not to decide every claim or submission.  
Check whether the facts, endorsements, and wording match the example closely enough to be useful.

**L.6.13** **Q:** How should you use a worked example that resembles your file?  
Use it to identify questions and possible coverage pathways.

**L.6.14** **A:** Do not assume the result will be identical.  
A small factual difference or a different endorsement can change the outcome.

**L.6.15** **Q:** What is a common risk when relying too heavily on a summary?  
You may overlook a condition, exception, definition, or exclusion.

**L.6.16** **A:** Summaries are helpful starting points, especially when you are learning.  
They are not substitutes for reading the operative wording.

**L.6.17** **Q:** How should you handle a customer question about the limit for any one tree, shrub or plant?  
Check the policy wording and any applicable endorsement before answering.

**L.6.18** **A:** Avoid treating a training summary as the final source for that issue.  
Your response should reflect the language that applies to the specific policy.

**L.6.19** **Q:** What should you do when a notice requirement is mentioned in guidance?  
Find the contract language that establishes the notice requirement and its effect.

**L.6.20** **A:** This is especially important when considering the deductible increase notice period.  
The wording may explain who must act, what must be delivered, and what follows if notice is disputed.

**L.6.21** **Q:** Why should you read definitions before applying a coverage summary?  
Definitions can give ordinary words a specialized meaning under the policy.

**L.6.22** **A:** A summary may use familiar language without repeating every defined term.  
The definition can determine whether the coverage grant applies at all.

**L.6.23** **Q:** Can you rely on a heading to decide coverage?  
No, headings help organize the policy but may not state the full rule.

**L.6.24** **A:** Read the complete provision, including related conditions and exceptions.  
A heading should guide your search, not replace your analysis.

**L.6.25** **Q:** What if guidance uses words such as usually, commonly, or may?  
Treat those words as signals that the outcome depends on facts and wording.

**L.6.26** **A:** They do not authorize you to skip the contract review.  
They remind you to look for the circumstances that affect the result.

**L.6.27** **Q:** How should you explain a coverage decision to a customer?  
Use clear language, but make sure your explanation follows the policy wording.

**L.6.28** **A:** State the relevant facts and the applicable contract provision.  
Do not describe guidance as though it were part of the customer’s policy.

**L.6.29** **Q:** What should you do if a colleague cites training material as the reason for a decision?  
Ask which contract language supports the conclusion.

**L.6.30** **A:** This keeps the discussion focused on the source that controls.  
It also helps identify whether the guidance was applied too broadly or too narrowly.

**L.6.31** **Q:** Can a checklist replace a review of the policy?  
No, a checklist helps you organize your work but does not answer every wording question.

**L.6.32** **A:** Use the checklist to gather facts and identify provisions to review.  
Then confirm the result against the actual contract language.

**L.6.33** **Q:** Why do endorsements require special attention?  
An endorsement can modify, remove, or add wording that changes the standard analysis.

**L.6.34** **A:** Guidance may describe a common form of coverage without reflecting every endorsement.  
Always verify which endorsements apply to the policy you are handling.

**L.6.35** **Q:** How should you handle a question about the payment after acceptance deadline?  
Locate the wording that addresses acceptance and payment before describing the requirement.

**L.6.36** **A:** Do not assume that a training explanation includes every trigger or exception.  
The contract language determines the parties’ obligations.

**L.6.37** **Q:** What if the policy language seems unclear after you read it?  
Do not fill the gap with an assumption based only on guidance.

**L.6.38** **A:** Document the issue, preserve the relevant facts, and seek appropriate review.  
Careful escalation is better than an unsupported interpretation.

**L.6.39** **Q:** Why is fact gathering important when guidance seems straightforward?  
Guidance often assumes facts that may not exist in your file.

**L.6.40** **A:** Ask questions that connect directly to the coverage wording.  
Your decision is only as reliable as the facts and contract terms supporting it.

**L.6.41** **Q:** How should you approach the roof inspection threshold?  
Identify the policy language that makes the inspection issue relevant.

**L.6.42** **A:** Confirm the property facts before reaching a conclusion.  
A general training statement may not address every applicable condition or exception.

**L.6.43** **Q:** Is it acceptable to use guidance when drafting a file note?  
Yes, if you use it to organize your analysis rather than as the authority for your conclusion.

**L.6.44** **A:** Your file note should identify the relevant facts and the contract wording applied.  
This makes your reasoning easier for another reviewer to follow.

**L.6.45** **Q:** What language should you avoid when explaining guidance to a customer?  
Avoid saying that guidance guarantees a result or replaces the policy.

**L.6.46** **A:** Instead, explain that the decision depends on the applicable wording and facts.  
Use direct language that does not overpromise coverage or payment.

**L.6.47** **Q:** What is the safest habit when guidance and policy wording seem to conflict?  
Pause and return to the wording that applies to the policy.

**L.6.48** **A:** Read the relevant provision in context before deciding what to communicate.  
If needed, obtain review through the proper internal process.

**L.6.49** **Q:** How can you tell whether you are using guidance appropriately?  
You can point to the policy wording that supports your conclusion.

**L.6.50** **A:** Guidance should help you reach the wording, not replace it.  
Your analysis should remain anchored in the contract.

**L.6.51** **Q:** What is the key lesson from this knowledge check?  
Use guidance to learn, prepare, and identify issues.

**L.6.52** **A:** Use contract language to make the coverage decision.  
When the sources differ, the contract language governs.
