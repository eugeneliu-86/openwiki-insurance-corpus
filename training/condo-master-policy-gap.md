# The Condominium Master Policy Gap







## L.1 — Learning Objectives

**L.1.1** You will learn to recognize the gap that can arise between condominium master policy coverage and the coverage held by a unit owner. You will be able to explain why that gap can affect underwriting decisions and claim handling.

**L.1.2** You will distinguish between property insured for the condominium corporation and property that may remain the responsibility of a unit owner. This distinction helps you identify where a loss may create competing expectations of coverage.

**L.1.3** You will learn to read the insuring agreement with attention to the property interest being protected. You should focus on who owns the damaged property, who is responsible for repair, and whose coverage responds.

**L.1.4** You will identify the importance of the condominium corporation’s governing requirements. Those requirements can shape the repair obligation even when the physical damage appears straightforward.

**L.1.5** You will recognize that common property, unit improvements, and original unit features may be treated differently. You should avoid assuming that visible property belongs within the same coverage category.

**L.1.6** You will learn to ask clear questions about how the unit was delivered, altered, and maintained. Those facts can affect whether damage falls within master policy coverage or leaves an uninsured exposure.

**L.1.7** You will understand how deductibles can create a practical gap after a covered loss. A deductible obligation may be assigned in a way that affects the condominium corporation, a unit owner, or another responsible party.

**L.1.8** You will identify situations where the master policy responds to damage but does not fully resolve the unit owner’s financial obligation. This helps you explain why coverage for assessments and improvements can matter.

**L.1.9** You will learn to separate damage to insured property from loss of use, extra expense, and personal property concerns. Each concern may require a different coverage analysis.

**L.1.10** You will recognize that the cause of loss matters as much as the location of the damage. You should confirm whether the event is covered before deciding which insured interest may be affected.

**L.1.11** You will learn to identify exclusions and limitations that may leave a gap even where the damaged property is otherwise insured. Careful analysis prevents you from treating a partial response as complete protection.

**L.1.12** You will understand the role of maintenance, wear, deterioration, and faulty work in a loss review. These conditions can change the coverage outcome and may point to responsibilities outside the master policy.

**L.1.13** You will recognize the need to gather information from the condominium corporation, property manager, unit owner, and relevant contractors. Consistent facts are essential when responsibility for repairs is unclear.

**L.1.14** You will learn to review the sequence of events without assuming that the first visible damage is the origin of the loss. The source of damage may be located beyond the area where the loss is discovered.

**L.1.15** You will identify how water damage can create difficult master policy gap questions. You should consider the source of water, the property damaged, and the repair obligation separately.

**L.1.16** You will understand that a claim can involve more than one insured party with different interests. Clear communication helps each party understand what is being reviewed and why.

**L.1.17** You will learn to explain coverage decisions in plain language without promising an outcome before the facts are confirmed. Your explanation should identify the issue, the information needed, and the next step in the review.

**L.1.18** You will recognize when a unit owner may need to look to separate coverage for property or expenses not addressed by the master policy. This supports accurate expectations without shifting responsibility prematurely.

**L.1.19** You will learn to identify underwriting information that can signal a greater chance of a master policy gap. Building features, maintenance practices, unit alterations, and claim history can all require closer attention.

**L.1.20** You will understand how clear records support a fair and efficient claim decision. Notes should show the facts considered, the coverage issue identified, and the basis for the response.

**L.1.21** You will recognize when the available information is incomplete or inconsistent. In those situations, you should request clarification rather than relying on assumptions about ownership or responsibility.

**L.1.22** By the end of this material, you should be able to identify likely master policy gaps and communicate them clearly. You will also be prepared to gather the facts needed for a sound underwriting or claim review.

## L.2 — Key Concepts

**L.2.1** **Q:** What is a condominium master policy gap? Why should you care about it when reviewing a loss?

**A:** A gap can arise when the association policy and the unit owner policy do not respond in the same way. Your job is to identify which policy may address each part of the loss.

**L.2.2** **Q:** Does the master policy protect every part of a unit? Can you assume the answer from the word “master”?

**A:** No. The master policy may protect some building property, while the unit owner policy may protect property the master policy does not cover. Read the policy language before deciding who is responsible.

**L.2.3** **Q:** What should you review before deciding whether there is a gap? Is the declaration important?

**A:** Start with the association declaration, the master policy, and the unit owner policy. These documents can describe different responsibilities for the same damaged property.

**L.2.4** **Q:** Why can unit boundaries create coverage questions? Do they always match what you can see inside the unit?

**A:** A unit boundary may be defined by legal language rather than by walls, floors, or ceilings you can easily identify. That can affect whether damaged property belongs to the association or the unit owner.

**L.2.5** **Q:** Can interior finishes be a source of disagreement? What makes them difficult?

**A:** Interior finishes may be treated differently under the master policy and the declaration. You should determine whether the association or the unit owner is responsible for restoring them.

**L.2.6** **Q:** What are betterments and improvements? Why do they matter in a condominium loss?

**A:** Betterments and improvements are changes that add value or alter the unit from its original condition. They may create a coverage gap if the master policy does not insure them.

**L.2.7** **Q:** Can an upgraded cabinet create a gap? Does it matter who installed it?

**A:** An upgraded cabinet can create a gap when it is not included in the property insured by the master policy. Installation history may help explain whether it is association property or unit owner property.

**L.2.8** **Q:** Should you assume original construction is always covered by the association? Is that a safe shortcut?

**A:** No. The declaration and master policy may limit what the association must insure. A shortcut can cause you to overlook a unit owner responsibility.

**L.2.9** **Q:** Can flooring be treated differently from other building property? What should you ask?

**A:** Flooring may be subject to special ownership or insurance rules. Ask whether it was part of the original construction and whether it has been replaced or upgraded.

**L.2.10** **Q:** Why are walls important in a condominium claim? Are all walls treated alike?

**A:** Walls can separate common property from unit property, and the policy may describe them differently. You should identify the damaged portion rather than relying on its location alone.

**L.2.11** **Q:** Can drywall create a master policy gap? What information helps resolve it?

**A:** Drywall may be covered differently depending on the unit boundary definition. The declaration, repair scope, and policy wording can help you determine responsibility.

**L.2.12** **Q:** What should you do when ceiling damage extends across several units? Can you treat the whole repair as unit owner property?

**A:** Do not assume the repair belongs entirely to the unit owners. Separate the damaged components and compare them with the association’s insurance obligations.

**L.2.13** **Q:** Does damage from a leaking pipe always belong under the master policy? What else must you consider?

**A:** The source of water and the damaged property may be treated separately. A pipe may be association property while the resulting damage affects property insured by a unit owner.

**L.2.14** **Q:** Why should you distinguish the cause of loss from the damaged property? Can the same policy answer both questions?

**A:** Coverage for the cause of loss and coverage for the damaged property can involve different policy provisions. You should analyze each issue without assuming the same policy responds to both.

**L.2.15** **Q:** Can a common element cause damage inside a unit? Does that automatically make the association responsible for all repairs?

**A:** A common element can cause damage inside a unit without making the association responsible for every damaged item. The declaration and insurance terms determine what property each party must insure.

**L.2.16** **Q:** What is a limited common element? Why should you identify it carefully?

**A:** A limited common element is property reserved for the use of a particular unit or group of units. Its ownership and insurance responsibility may differ from property used by the entire association.

**L.2.17** **Q:** Can a balcony be a limited common element? What coverage question does that raise?

**A:** A balcony may be treated as a limited common element under the declaration. You should determine whether the association policy, the unit owner policy, or another arrangement addresses the damage.

**L.2.18** **Q:** Can windows create a coverage gap? Why are they often disputed?

**A:** Windows can be part of the building while also serving a particular unit. The declaration may assign maintenance and insurance duties differently.

**L.2.19** **Q:** What should you do when the association says a unit owner must maintain property? Does maintenance responsibility decide insurance coverage?

**A:** Maintenance responsibility does not always determine insurance responsibility. Review the insurance provisions rather than treating a maintenance rule as a coverage answer.

**L.2.20** **Q:** Can the association be responsible for insurance but not repairs? Why does that distinction matter?

**A:** Insurance obligations and repair obligations can be assigned differently. You should avoid assuming that the party arranging insurance must perform every repair.

**L.2.21** **Q:** What is a deductible assessment? Why might it matter to a unit owner?

**A:** A deductible assessment can occur when the association charges unit owners for a share of the master policy deductible. A unit owner policy may provide coverage only if its terms apply to that assessment.

**L.2.22** **Q:** Is every assessment covered by a unit owner policy? What should you verify?

**A:** No. An assessment must meet the policy requirements before coverage applies. Review the reason for the assessment, the damaged property, and the applicable coverage wording.

**L.2.23** **Q:** What is loss assessment coverage? How does it help with condominium gaps?

**A:** Loss assessment coverage may help when an association assesses a unit owner for certain covered losses. It does not replace careful review of the master policy and declaration.

**L.2.24** **Q:** How much can loss assessment coverage pay? Where should you find the answer?

**A:** The most we will pay is $25,000 (twenty-five thousand dollars). You should still confirm that the assessment meets all applicable coverage conditions.

**L.2.25** **Q:** Can a master policy deductible be assessed after a covered loss? Does the assessment itself establish coverage?

**A:** A master policy deductible may be assessed under the association’s governing rules. The fact that an assessment was made does not by itself establish unit owner coverage.

**L.2.26** **Q:** Why should you request the assessment notice? Is an explanation from the insured enough?

**A:** The assessment notice can show the reason, property involved, and association action. An insured’s explanation is useful, but it may not provide the full coverage basis.

**L.2.27** **Q:** Can an assessment arise from damage outside the insured unit? Why is that possible?

**A:** An association may assess unit owners for certain shared obligations. The damaged property may be outside the insured unit even though the unit owner receives an assessment.

**L.2.28** **Q:** Does an assessment for routine maintenance create a covered loss assessment? What should you avoid?

**A:** Routine maintenance is not automatically a covered loss assessment. Avoid treating every association charge as insurance-related damage.

**L.2.29** **Q:** Can wear and tear lead to an assessment? Does that mean the unit owner policy will respond?

**A:** Wear and tear can lead to association expenses and assessments. Coverage depends on the policy terms and is not established merely because the unit owner must contribute.

**L.2.30** **Q:** Why does the reason for an assessment matter? Can the same association charge have different causes?

**A:** The reason for the assessment helps determine whether the policy may respond. Similar charges can arise from very different events and obligations.

**L.2.31** **Q:** What should you look for in the master policy property definition? Why is the label important?

**A:** Look for language describing building property, common property, and unit property. Labels can control whether a damaged item is insured by the association.

**L.2.32** **Q:** What should you look for in the declaration’s insurance provision? Can you rely only on the master policy?

**A:** Look for language assigning insurance responsibilities between the association and unit owners. The master policy alone may not explain the full allocation of responsibility.

**L.2.33** **Q:** Can the declaration require the association to insure certain improvements? What should you do if it does?

**A:** The declaration may require the association to insure property that otherwise appears to be unit owner property. Compare that requirement with the actual master policy wording.

**L.2.34** **Q:** What if the declaration and master policy seem inconsistent? Should you resolve the conflict by assumption?

**A:** Do not resolve an apparent conflict by assumption. Document the issue and seek the guidance required by your claim or underwriting process.

**L.2.35** **Q:** Why should you identify the date of loss? Can later repairs change the analysis?

**A:** The applicable policy and governing provisions may depend on the circumstances in effect at the time of loss. Later repairs can help explain the condition of the property but may not change the original coverage analysis.

**L.2.36** **Q:** Can a renovation completed before the loss affect coverage? What records can help?

**A:** A renovation can affect whether property is original construction, an improvement, or personal property. Invoices, photographs, and association approvals can help clarify the issue.

**L.2.37** **Q:** Why should you ask who paid for an upgrade? Does payment settle ownership?

**A:** Payment can help explain the history of an upgrade. It does not always settle ownership or insurance responsibility.

**L.2.38** **Q:** Can personal property be confused with building property? What is a useful way to separate them?

**A:** Personal property is generally movable, while building property is commonly attached or integrated into the unit. You should examine the item and the policy definitions rather than relying only on appearance.

**L.2.39** **Q:** Can an appliance be building property in a condominium? Why should you avoid a quick answer?

**A:** An appliance may be treated differently depending on how it is installed and what the policies say. Avoid deciding based only on whether the appliance is inside the unit.

**L.2.40** **Q:** Why are fixtures important in a master policy gap review? Can they be owned differently from the unit itself?

**A:** Fixtures can be tied to the building while still raising unit ownership questions. The declaration and policy wording may assign them to different insurance responsibilities.

**L.2.41** **Q:** Can a built-in feature be excluded from the master policy? What should you compare?

**A:** A built-in feature may fall outside the master policy’s insured property definition. Compare the feature with the declaration and the unit owner policy’s building property coverage.

**L.2.42** **Q:** What does restoration mean in a condominium loss? Does it always mean upgrading the unit?

**A:** Restoration generally concerns repairing or replacing damaged property under the applicable coverage terms. It does not automatically mean improving the unit beyond its preloss condition.

**L.2.43** **Q:** Can matching concerns create a gap? Why should you identify undamaged materials?

**A:** Matching concerns can arise when covered repairs affect the appearance of nearby undamaged materials. Identify the damaged property and the repair scope before deciding what coverage may apply.

**L.2.44** **Q:** Should you treat an association repair decision as a coverage decision? Why not?

**A:** An association may choose a repair approach for operational or practical reasons. That choice does not by itself establish what an insurance policy covers.

**L.2.45** **Q:** Can a unit owner pay for repairs before coverage is decided? What information should you obtain?

**A:** A unit owner may make repairs to protect the property from further damage. Obtain records showing what was repaired, why the work was needed, and what property was involved.

**L.2.46** **Q:** Why should you separate emergency work from permanent repairs? Can they involve different property?

**A:** Emergency work may address immediate conditions, while permanent repairs restore damaged property. The work can involve different components with different insurance responsibilities.

**L.2.47** **Q:** Can mitigation expenses create the same gap issues as repair costs? What should you review?

**A:** Mitigation work can affect common property, unit property, and personal property. Review the work performed and the policy provisions that apply to each category.

**L.2.48** **Q:** What should you do when damage crosses a unit boundary? Is a single repair estimate enough?

**A:** Identify the damaged portions on each side of the boundary. A repair estimate can be useful, but it may need to be separated by property responsibility.

**L.2.49** **Q:** Can damage in a hallway affect a unit owner claim? Why might the unit owner still be involved?

**A:** Hallway damage may lead to an association action or assessment affecting unit owners. The unit owner’s involvement does not make the hallway part of the insured unit.

**L.2.50** **Q:** Can common area damage lead to loss assessment coverage? What must you confirm?

**A:** Common area damage can lead to an assessment under some circumstances. Confirm the reason for the assessment and the policy requirements before reaching a conclusion.

**L.2.51** **Q:** Why should you ask whether the association has filed a claim? Does that answer every question?

**A:** An association claim can reveal how the master policy is being used. It does not answer whether the unit owner policy covers separate property or an assessment.

**L.2.52** **Q:** What if the association chooses not to file a master policy claim? Can you assume there is no covered loss?

**A:** An association may choose not to file for reasons unrelated to coverage. You should still evaluate the unit owner claim under its own policy terms.

**L.2.53** **Q:** Can the master policy insurer deny coverage while the unit owner policy responds? Why is coordination important?

**A:** Different policies can reach different coverage outcomes because they insure different interests and property. Coordinate information without assuming that a denial under one policy controls the other.

**L.2.54** **Q:** Can the unit owner policy deny coverage while the master policy responds? What does that teach you?

**A:** Yes. A master policy may cover association property even when the unit owner policy does not cover the unit owner’s claimed property.

**L.2.55** **Q:** Why should you avoid promising that a gap will be filled? What should you say instead?

**A:** Do not promise coverage before reviewing the applicable terms and facts. Explain that you are determining which policy may respond to each part of the loss.

**L.2.56** **Q:** How can you explain a gap to an insured without sounding dismissive? What tone should you use?

**A:** Use clear language and acknowledge that condominium insurance can be confusing. Explain that the association and unit owner may have separate responsibilities for the same event.

**L.2.57** **Q:** What should you document when a gap is identified? Why does documentation matter?

**A:** Document the damaged property, the ownership issue, the applicable policy language, and the information received from the association. Clear documentation supports a consistent coverage decision.

**L.2.58** **Q:** Should you rely on verbal statements from association staff? What is a better approach?

**A:** Verbal statements can provide useful leads, but they may be incomplete or inaccurate. Request written governing provisions, repair information, and assessment records when available.

**L.2.59** **Q:** Why are photographs useful in a condominium loss? Can photographs determine coverage by themselves?

**A:** Photographs can show the location, condition, and apparent nature of damaged property. They do not replace the legal and policy analysis needed to determine coverage.

**L.2.60** **Q:** Can repair invoices help identify a gap? What should you look for in them?

**A:** Repair invoices can identify materials, labor, and the areas involved in the work. Look for details that help separate common property, unit property, and personal property.

**L.2.61** **Q:** Why should you review association meeting records? What might they reveal?

**A:** Association records may explain the cause of an assessment or the association’s repair decision. They can also show whether the charge relates to a covered event or an operational expense.

**L.2.62** **Q:** Can a special assessment be unrelated to a covered loss? Why is that distinction important?

**A:** A special assessment may be imposed for many reasons. You must identify the actual reason rather than treating the assessment label as proof of coverage.

**L.2.63** **Q:** Can an assessment for a capital project be confused with loss assessment coverage? What should you remember?

**A:** A capital project can involve planned improvement or replacement rather than accidental direct physical loss. The existence of an assessment does not make the project an insured loss.

**L.2.64** **Q:** Why should you ask whether the damage was sudden or gradual? Can that affect the gap review?

**A:** The nature of the damage can affect whether either policy responds. It can also affect whether the association treats the expense as a repair obligation or an assessment.

**L.2.65** **Q:** Can repeated leakage create different issues than a sudden leak? What should you investigate?

**A:** Repeated leakage may involve maintenance, deterioration, or prior damage concerns. Investigate the source, duration, prior reports, and damaged property.

**L.2.66** **Q:** What if a contractor calls all damaged material “building damage”? Should you accept that label?

**A:** A contractor’s description can help identify the repair scope. It does not determine ownership or insurance responsibility under the policies.

**L.2.67** **Q:** Can a unit owner’s lease affect the master policy gap? Why should you know who occupies the unit?

**A:** Occupancy can affect the information available and the interests involved in the claim. It does not change the need to identify the property insured by each policy.

**L.2.68** **Q:** Does a tenant’s personal property become association property after a building loss? What is the key point?

**A:** No. A building loss can damage several types of property without changing who owns them. Keep tenant property separate from association and unit owner building property.

**L.2.69** **Q:** Can damage to a shared utility line create a difficult claim? What should you identify?

**A:** Shared utility lines can serve common property and individual units. Identify the line’s location, function, ownership, and the property damaged by the failure.

**L.2.70** **Q:** Why should you distinguish access damage from source damage? Can they fall in different coverage categories?

**A:** Access damage can occur when property must be opened to reach a source of loss. The source and the access damage may involve different policy provisions and insured property.

**L.2.71** **Q:** Can an association’s repair standard exceed policy coverage? What should you avoid telling the insured?

**A:** An association may require work that exceeds the covered repair scope. Avoid telling the insured that every required repair is automatically covered.

**L.2.72** **Q:** Can code requirements affect a condominium repair? Why should you gather details?

**A:** Code requirements can affect the repair scope and the parties involved in restoration. Gather the relevant details before deciding how the coverage applies.

**L.2.73** **Q:** What if the association repairs common property while the unit owner repairs interior finishes? Is that unusual?

**A:** That division of work can occur when the declaration and policies assign separate responsibilities. Treat each repair category according to the applicable facts and wording.

**L.2.74** **Q:** Can a unit owner have coverage for property the association also insures? Why must you avoid assumptions?

**A:** Policies can overlap in some situations, but overlap does not mean duplicate payment. Review the applicable policy provisions and coordinate the information carefully.

**L.2.75** **Q:** Why should you avoid deciding coverage from a condominium marketing description? What controls instead?

**A:** Marketing descriptions may not reflect legal boundaries or insurance obligations. The declaration and policy language control the coverage analysis.

**L.2.76** **Q:** Can an informal association rule change insurance coverage? What should you request?

**A:** An informal rule may explain how the association handles repairs, but it may not change the governing insurance obligations. Request the written authority supporting the association’s position.

**L.2.77** **Q:** What is the practical goal of a master policy gap review? Is it only to find a denial?

**A:** The goal is to identify the proper policy response for each part of the loss. A careful review can reveal coverage, limitations, shared responsibilities, or an uncovered expense.

**L.2.78** **Q:** How should you organize your claim file when several parties are involved? What makes the file easier to review?

**A:** Separate information about the association, the unit owner, damaged property, repairs, and assessments. A clear file helps you explain how each coverage question was evaluated.

**L.2.79** **Q:** What is the best habit when handling a possible condominium master policy gap? How can you keep the analysis accurate?

**A:** Slow down and identify the damaged property before deciding which policy applies. Compare the governing provisions and policy language rather than relying on labels or assumptions.

## L.3 — Worked Examples

**L.3.1** **Q:** What is a condominium master policy gap? **A:** It is the difference between what the association coverage pays and what remains your unit owner’s responsibility.

**L.3.2** **Q:** Why does this gap matter after a loss? **A:** A loss can affect building property, unit improvements, personal property, and loss assessments in different ways.

**L.3.3** **Q:** Does the master policy always insure the entire unit? **A:** No, the master policy may stop at a boundary that leaves some unit property uninsured by that coverage.

**L.3.4** **Q:** What should you identify before evaluating the gap? **A:** Identify the damaged property, the cause of loss, and the party responsible for restoring it.

**L.3.5** **Q:** Is a damaged wall always the association’s responsibility? **A:** No, responsibility can depend on whether the wall is common property, original unit property, or a unit improvement.

**L.3.6** **Q:** What is an original unit finish? **A:** It is property that was part of the unit when the relevant coverage boundary was established.

**L.3.7** **Q:** What is a unit improvement? **A:** It is property added, altered, or upgraded for the unit beyond its original condition.

**L.3.8** **Q:** Why do improvements create coverage issues? **A:** The association coverage may not respond to all upgrades made within a unit.

**L.3.9** **Q:** Can a kitchen loss involve more than one coverage interest? **A:** Yes, cabinets, appliances, flooring, and related damage may fall into different responsibility categories.

**L.3.10** **Q:** What is the underwriter’s basic task? **A:** You match the unit owner’s potential responsibility to the protection available under the unit policy.

**L.3.11** **Q:** What is the adjuster’s basic task? **A:** You determine what was damaged and whether the claimed property falls within the unit owner’s coverage.

**L.3.12** **Q:** Does an association payment end the analysis? **A:** No, you still determine whether any unpaid responsibility remains with the unit owner.

**L.3.13** **Q:** What if the association coverage pays for part of the repair? **A:** You examine the remaining repair obligation before deciding whether a gap exists.

**L.3.14** **Q:** Can the unit owner be responsible for a deductible? **A:** Yes, a deductible may be passed through when the unit owner is responsible under the applicable arrangement.

**L.3.15** **Q:** Is every passed-through deductible covered by the unit policy? **A:** No, coverage depends on the applicable insuring agreement, exclusions, and conditions.

**L.3.16** **Q:** What should you ask about a loss assessment? **A:** Ask what caused the assessment and what property or expense the assessment represents.

**L.3.17** **Q:** Why is the reason for an assessment important? **A:** An assessment tied to covered property damage may be treated differently from an assessment for maintenance.

**L.3.18** **Q:** Is routine maintenance a sudden accidental loss? **A:** No, routine maintenance is generally not treated as direct physical loss.

**L.3.19** **Q:** Can deferred repairs create a covered loss assessment? **A:** Not automatically, because the reason for the charge still controls the coverage analysis.

**L.3.20** **Q:** What does direct physical loss mean in this context? **A:** It means tangible damage to property from a covered cause of loss.

**L.3.21** **Q:** Does worn property become covered because it finally fails? **A:** No, wear, deterioration, and similar conditions can affect the result.

**L.3.22** **Q:** What if water damages a unit after a pipe leak? **A:** Separate the resulting water damage from the cause of the leak and evaluate each issue carefully.

**L.3.23** **Q:** Is the source of water always the only issue? **A:** No, the damaged property and the responsible party also matter.

**L.3.24** **Q:** How should you handle a ceiling stain from an upper unit? **A:** Determine whether the stain reflects covered damage and whether the repair belongs to the association or a unit owner.

**L.3.25** **Q:** What if the upper unit owner caused the damage? **A:** Liability issues may arise, but property coverage must still be analyzed under its own terms.

**L.3.26** **Q:** Does negligence automatically create property coverage? **A:** No, negligence may establish responsibility without changing the coverage requirements.

**L.3.27** **Q:** Can a claim involve property coverage and liability coverage? **A:** Yes, the same event can raise separate questions under different coverage parts.

**L.3.28** **Q:** What if a contractor damages a unit improvement? **A:** Identify the damaged improvement and determine whether the unit owner has an insured interest in it.

**L.3.29** **Q:** Why does ownership matter? **A:** Coverage generally requires that the insured have a covered interest in the damaged property.

**L.3.30** **Q:** What if the association owns the damaged property? **A:** The unit owner’s coverage may be limited unless the unit owner has a covered responsibility for it.

**L.3.31** **Q:** What is a common-property loss? **A:** It is a loss involving property shared by the condominium community rather than property used solely within a unit.

**L.3.32** **Q:** Can common-property damage affect a unit owner? **A:** Yes, the unit owner may face an assessment or may suffer related damage within the unit.

**L.3.33** **Q:** What if a shared pipe damages several units? **A:** Treat the shared pipe, resulting unit damage, and any assessment as related but separate issues.

**L.3.34** **Q:** Should you assume all damaged flooring is common property? **A:** No, flooring responsibility can vary based on its location and status as original property or an improvement.

**L.3.35** **Q:** How should you approach upgraded flooring? **A:** Treat it as a possible unit improvement and confirm whether the unit owner has coverage for that interest.

**L.3.36** **Q:** What if the upgrade was installed by a prior owner? **A:** Focus on the current unit owner’s interest and responsibility for the damaged property.

**L.3.37** **Q:** Does a unit owner need to have installed an improvement personally? **A:** No, the relevant issue is the insured interest at the time of loss.

**L.3.38** **Q:** What if the improvement is built into the unit? **A:** Built-in property can still require careful classification between building property and personal property.

**L.3.39** **Q:** Are built-in appliances always treated the same way? **A:** No, their treatment can depend on ownership, attachment, and the applicable coverage terms.

**L.3.40** **Q:** What is the purpose of Coverage A in this setting? **A:** Coverage A can help address the unit owner’s interest in the dwelling and related building property.

**L.3.41** **Q:** What limit applies by default to Coverage A? **A:** The default Coverage A limit is $10,000 (ten thousand dollars).

**L.3.42** **Q:** Does the Coverage A limit solve every master policy gap? **A:** No, the available limit is only part of the coverage analysis.

**L.3.43** **Q:** What else matters besides the Coverage A limit? **A:** Covered cause of loss, property classification, exclusions, deductibles, and conditions can all matter.

**L.3.44** **Q:** Why should you avoid treating Coverage A as blanket protection? **A:** A dwelling limit does not remove the need to identify the insured property and covered cause of loss.

**L.3.45** **Q:** What if the cost to restore an improvement is substantial? **A:** Consider whether the unit owner’s dwelling coverage is designed for that improvement exposure.

**L.3.46** **Q:** Can personal property coverage pay for damage to a built-in cabinet? **A:** Usually, you first determine whether the cabinet is building property rather than personal property.

**L.3.47** **Q:** What is personal property in a condominium claim? **A:** It is movable property owned or used by the insured, subject to the applicable terms.

**L.3.48** **Q:** Why distinguish personal property from building property? **A:** The coverage part, valuation approach, and available protection may differ.

**L.3.49** **Q:** What if water damages furniture and installed flooring? **A:** Evaluate the furniture as personal property and the flooring according to its building-property status.

**L.3.50** **Q:** Can a loss assessment be related to a master policy deductible? **A:** Yes, an assessment may arise when the association applies its deductible to a covered loss.

**L.3.51** **Q:** What should you determine about a deductible assessment? **A:** Determine whether the assessment resulted from covered property damage rather than an unrelated charge.

**L.3.52** **Q:** Why should you identify the cause of loss behind the deductible? **A:** The underlying cause can affect whether the unit owner’s coverage responds.

**L.3.53** **Q:** How does the all-other-perils deductible minimum affect the analysis? **A:** Treat the all-other-perils deductible minimum as a separate deductible consideration.

**L.3.54** **Q:** How does the named storm deductible minimum affect the analysis? **A:** Treat the named storm deductible minimum as a separate storm-related deductible consideration.

**L.3.55** **Q:** Why should storm losses be classified carefully? **A:** The cause of loss can determine which deductible treatment applies.

**L.3.56** **Q:** What if wind damages common roofing and water enters a unit? **A:** Separate the common-property damage from the interior unit damage while keeping the causes connected.

**L.3.57** **Q:** Can rain damage be covered when wind creates an opening? **A:** The answer depends on the applicable coverage wording and the established facts of the loss.

**L.3.58** **Q:** What if water enters without visible storm damage? **A:** Investigate the route of entry and avoid assuming that all interior water damage has the same cause.

**L.3.59** **Q:** Why is documentation important in a gap claim? **A:** Clear facts help you connect damaged property to the correct coverage interest.

**L.3.60** **Q:** What documentation is most useful? **A:** Photos, repair details, ownership information, and the basis for any assessment can clarify the claim.

**L.3.61** **Q:** What if a unit owner receives an assessment without repair details? **A:** Request enough information to understand what the assessment is intended to fund.

**L.3.62** **Q:** Can an assessment for landscaping create a dwelling claim? **A:** Not usually, because landscaping expenses do not automatically represent damage to the insured dwelling interest.

**L.3.63** **Q:** What if an assessment includes several types of expenses? **A:** Break the assessment into its components before applying coverage.

**L.3.64** **Q:** Why should you avoid treating an assessment as a single undivided charge? **A:** Different portions may arise from different causes, property interests, or coverage provisions.

**L.3.65** **Q:** What is a special assessment? **A:** It is a charge imposed on unit owners for a stated association expense or obligation.

**L.3.66** **Q:** Is every special assessment a covered loss assessment? **A:** No, the label of the charge does not determine coverage.

**L.3.67** **Q:** What if the association assesses unit owners for a policy deductible? **A:** Determine the cause of loss, the damaged property, and the unit owner’s responsibility for the charge.

**L.3.68** **Q:** Can a deductible assessment involve a unit owner who did not cause the loss? **A:** Yes, an assessment can affect unit owners even when they did not cause the damage.

**L.3.69** **Q:** Does lack of fault prevent a property claim? **A:** No, property coverage can depend on covered loss and insured interest rather than fault.

**L.3.70** **Q:** What if the unit owner caused the water loss? **A:** Consider the property claim separately from any liability exposure arising from the event.

**L.3.71** **Q:** What if damage spreads from a unit to common property? **A:** Identify all damaged property and avoid assuming a single coverage part handles every item.

**L.3.72** **Q:** What if common property damage spreads into a unit? **A:** Identify which damaged items are part of the unit owner’s insured interest.

**L.3.73** **Q:** How do you handle cabinetry damaged by a common pipe failure? **A:** Determine whether the cabinetry is original unit property, an improvement, or property for which another party is responsible.

**L.3.74** **Q:** What if the unit owner installed custom cabinetry? **A:** Treat the cabinetry as a potential improvement and evaluate the dwelling coverage available for it.

**L.3.75** **Q:** How do you handle a damaged interior door? **A:** Determine whether the door is within the unit owner’s insured building-property interest.

**L.3.76** **Q:** What if a common hallway is damaged near the unit entrance? **A:** Do not assume the unit owner’s dwelling coverage applies to shared-area property.

**L.3.77** **Q:** Can damage to an exterior wall affect the unit owner? **A:** Yes, it can lead to interior damage or an assessment, even if the wall itself is common property.

**L.3.78** **Q:** What if mold is discovered after a water event? **A:** Examine the water event, the resulting damage, and any applicable limitations or exclusions.

**L.3.79** **Q:** Why should you separate cause from consequence? **A:** The triggering event and later conditions may receive different treatment under the coverage.

**L.3.80** **Q:** What if a sump system overflows into the unit? **A:** Consider whether the water backup and sump overflow sublimit applies to the reported damage.

**L.3.81** **Q:** Does every water-related loss involve water backup coverage? **A:** No, water can enter or damage property through different mechanisms.

**L.3.82** **Q:** What if a sewer line backs up into a lower-level unit? **A:** Identify the source of the water and the damaged property before selecting the relevant coverage path.

**L.3.83** **Q:** What if theft occurs from a unit during repairs? **A:** Treat theft as its own cause of loss and identify the stolen property.

**L.3.84** **Q:** How should you handle jewelry stolen from a unit? **A:** Consider whether the precious stones theft limit affects the claimed property.

**L.3.85** **Q:** Is stolen jewelry part of the master policy gap? **A:** It can be a unit-owner exposure, but it is not building damage simply because it occurred in a condominium.

**L.3.86** **Q:** What if tools are stolen from a unit renovation project? **A:** Identify who owned the tools and whether they qualify as covered personal property.

**L.3.87** **Q:** Can contractor property be treated as the unit owner’s personal property? **A:** No, ownership and insured status must be established before coverage is considered.

**L.3.88** **Q:** What does coinsurance threshold mean for a unit owner? **A:** The coinsurance threshold can affect how you evaluate whether the dwelling amount supports the insured property interest.

**L.3.89** **Q:** Why should underwriting consider unit improvements early? **A:** Improvements can increase the gap between the unit owner’s responsibility and the association coverage.

**L.3.90** **Q:** What information helps identify improvement exposure? **A:** Information about renovations, built-in upgrades, and altered finishes helps you understand the dwelling risk.

**L.3.91** **Q:** What if a unit owner cannot describe prior upgrades? **A:** Use available claim facts to identify visible improvements and avoid assumptions about their value.

**L.3.92** **Q:** How should you explain a master policy gap to a unit owner? **A:** Explain that association protection and unit-owner protection may not insure the same property interests.

**L.3.93** **Q:** What is a clear way to explain a coverage boundary? **A:** Say that the association may insure some building property while the unit owner may remain responsible for other property.

**L.3.94** **Q:** What should you avoid when explaining the gap? **A:** Avoid promising that either coverage will pay before the damaged property and cause of loss are known.

**L.3.95** **Q:** What if a claimant believes the association should pay everything? **A:** Acknowledge the concern and explain that responsibility can differ by property type and cause of loss.

**L.3.96** **Q:** What if a claimant believes the unit policy should pay everything inside the unit? **A:** Explain that property inside a unit can include personal property, building property, and property belonging to others.

**L.3.97** **Q:** What is the best claim-handling sequence? **A:** Identify the event, classify the damaged property, determine the unit owner’s interest, and apply the relevant coverage terms.

**L.3.98** **Q:** What is the best underwriting sequence? **A:** Identify the likely unit-owner responsibility for building property and consider whether the dwelling coverage addresses that exposure.

**L.3.99** **Q:** What is the central lesson of the master policy gap? **A:** The gap is not a single item of property but a coverage problem created by differing responsibilities.

**L.3.100** **Q:** What should you remember when a condominium claim seems simple? **A:** Slow down, classify the property, identify the cause, and determine what responsibility remains with the unit owner.

## L.4 — Common Mistakes

**L.4.1** **Q:** Is it safe to assume the association’s master policy covers everything inside your home?

**A:** No. The master policy may stop at certain building components, while your personal policy may need to respond to interior items.

Read the coverage descriptions closely. Do not rely only on what the association calls “full coverage.”

**L.4.2** **Q:** Can you treat all condominium associations the same way?

**A:** No. Associations may have different responsibilities for walls, flooring, fixtures, improvements, and common property.

Ask what the association insures before deciding what coverage you need for the unit owner.

**L.4.3** **Q:** Is a bare walls description always easy to apply?

**A:** No. Bare walls can sound simple, but disagreements often arise over cabinets, flooring, appliances, plumbing fixtures, and interior finishes.

Clarify which property belongs to the association and which property belongs to the unit owner.

**L.4.4** **Q:** Does original construction always belong to the association?

**A:** Not always. Some association documents place responsibility for original fixtures or finishes on the unit owner.

Do not assume that an item is covered just because it was present when the building was completed.

**L.4.5** **Q:** Are improvements and betterments automatically covered by the master policy?

**A:** No. Improvements and betterments may be the unit owner’s responsibility, even when they blend into the original construction.

Treat upgraded flooring, cabinetry, wall finishes, and built-in features as items requiring careful review.

**L.4.6** **Q:** Can a claim handler assume that installed property belongs to the association?

**A:** No. Installed property can be owned by the unit owner, the association, or another party depending on the governing arrangement.

Ownership matters because it affects which policy should respond to the loss.

**L.4.7** **Q:** Is the association responsible for every water damage claim?

**A:** No. The source of the water, the damaged property, and the association’s maintenance duties can all affect coverage.

Separate the cause of loss from the question of who owned the damaged property.

**L.4.8** **Q:** Does damage from a neighboring unit automatically become the neighbor’s insurance problem?

**A:** No. The damaged unit owner may need to use personal coverage, and fault may be resolved later.

Do not promise recovery from another owner before the facts establish legal responsibility.

**L.4.9** **Q:** Can you assume the association will repair interior finishes after a common-property loss?

**A:** No. The association may repair the common property while the unit owner remains responsible for restoring finishes inside the unit.

Explain this distinction early so the insured understands why more than one policy may be involved.

**L.4.10** **Q:** Is the deductible always paid by the association?

**A:** No. The association may allocate its deductible to a unit owner when that owner is responsible under the applicable rules.

Review the loss facts and the association’s allocation process before deciding whether personal coverage may apply.

**L.4.11** **Q:** Does an association deductible allocation mean the unit owner caused the loss?

**A:** No. An allocation can occur without proving that the unit owner was negligent.

Keep the coverage question separate from the fault question.

**L.4.12** **Q:** Can you call every deductible charge an assessment?

**A:** No. A deductible allocation and an association assessment can be handled differently under the coverage wording.

Use the association’s description, but confirm what the charge actually represents.

**L.4.13** **Q:** Is any charge from the association covered as a loss assessment?

**A:** No. A charge must fit the coverage wording before it can be treated as covered.

A special charge, maintenance expense, penalty, or repair bill may not have the same treatment as a covered assessment.

**L.4.14** **Q:** Should you ignore the association assessment limit until after the claim is settled?

**A:** No. The association assessment limit may affect how you explain the available protection from the start.

Identify the nature of the assessment before discussing how the limit may apply.

**L.4.15** **Q:** Does a special assessment always arise from a covered property loss?

**A:** No. Associations can levy special assessments for many reasons, including maintenance, upgrades, operating needs, and uninsured damage.

Ask what caused the assessment and why the association issued it.

**L.4.16** **Q:** Can you assume an assessment is covered because the association suffered damage?

**A:** No. The assessment must still arise from a cause and circumstance addressed by the applicable coverage.

Focus on the reason for the charge, not merely the fact that the association had a loss.

**L.4.17** **Q:** Does the master policy settle every dispute between the association and the unit owner?

**A:** No. Coverage decisions do not resolve all ownership, maintenance, or contractual disputes.

Avoid presenting an insurance response as a final answer to every disagreement within the condominium community.

**L.4.18** **Q:** Is damage from seepage always handled the same as sudden water damage?

**A:** No. The timing, source, condition of the property, and coverage wording may lead to different results.

Gather facts about when the problem was discovered and what allowed the damage to develop.

**L.4.19** **Q:** Can mold-related damage be treated as ordinary water damage without further review?

**A:** No. Mold-related issues may involve separate conditions, exclusions, or sublimits.

Review whether the fungi and bacteria aggregate affects the available response.

**L.4.20** **Q:** Does building code work automatically become part of the repair cost?

**A:** No. Required code-related work can have different treatment from direct physical damage repairs.

Consider whether the ordinance or law additional amount applies to the necessary work.

**L.4.21** **Q:** Can you promise that code upgrades will be covered whenever a damaged item is replaced?

**A:** No. Coverage for code-related costs depends on the cause of loss, the damaged property, and the applicable wording.

Do not describe a required upgrade as covered until the coverage basis is clear.

**L.4.22** **Q:** Is the unit owner’s personal property protected by the master policy?

**A:** Usually not. Furniture, clothing, electronics, and other personal belongings are generally the unit owner’s insurance concern.

Do not confuse personal property with fixtures or building property.

**L.4.23** **Q:** Can you assume a fixture is personal property because it can be removed?

**A:** No. A removable item may still be treated as part of the building depending on how it is installed and used.

Ask whether removing the item would damage the unit or change its intended function.

**L.4.24** **Q:** Is loss of use handled by the association’s master policy?

**A:** Not usually. A unit owner may need personal coverage for added living costs or lost rental income after a covered loss.

Confirm whether the unit was occupied by the owner, occupied by a tenant, or held for rental use.

**L.4.25** **Q:** Can an adjuster wait to request claim documentation?

**A:** No. Delayed documentation can make it harder to establish ownership, cause of loss, repair scope, and assessment details.

Explain the proof of loss period clearly when requesting information from the insured.

**L.4.26** **Q:** Is it acceptable to delay a coverage decision while waiting for unrelated association issues?

**A:** No. You should continue investigating the facts needed for the coverage decision.

Separate unresolved community disputes from information that is actually necessary to evaluate the claim.

**L.4.27** **Q:** Can you overlook a lawsuit because claim discussions are continuing?

**A:** No. Ongoing discussions do not necessarily change the legal action deadline.

Escalate legal papers promptly and avoid suggesting that informal negotiations preserve every right.

**L.4.28** **Q:** Does a nonrenewal notice have anything to do with claim handling?

**A:** It can. A notice of nonrenewal may affect the insured’s concerns about future protection, even when it does not decide the current claim.

Address the current loss accurately without making promises about future coverage.

**L.4.29** **Q:** What is the most common communication mistake in condominium claims?

**A:** Giving a confident answer before confirming who owns the damaged property, who maintains it, and what caused the loss.

Use clear language, explain what remains under review, and avoid assuming the master policy fills every gap.

## L.6 — Knowledge Check

**L.6.1 Q:** What is the main purpose of a condominium master policy? How does it relate to unit-owner coverage?

**L.6.2 A:** The master policy commonly protects property and interests held by the association. Unit-owner coverage may respond to property or obligations that remain with the individual owner.

**L.6.3 Q:** Why can a unit owner have a coverage gap after a covered building loss? Isn’t the association responsible for the building?

**L.6.4 A:** The association may be responsible only for property within its defined responsibility. A gap can arise when the owner, association, and insurer do not treat the damaged property the same way.

**L.6.5 Q:** What should you identify before deciding whether a damaged item belongs to the association or the owner? What makes this important?

**L.6.6 A:** Identify the property boundary described by the association’s controlling terms and the applicable coverage. This helps you avoid assuming that an interior item is automatically association property.

**L.6.7 Q:** Are interior walls always part of the master policy’s responsibility? Can finishes change the answer?

**L.6.8 A:** Interior walls may fall within the master policy, owner coverage, or a shared responsibility depending on the applicable terms. Finishes, fixtures, and improvements can make the property decision more complex.

**L.6.9 Q:** What are betterments and improvements in a condominium claim? Why should you ask about them?

**L.6.10 A:** Betterments and improvements are changes that increase or alter the unit beyond its original condition. You should ask about them because responsibility for those changes may rest with the unit owner.

**L.6.11 Q:** Can an association assessment create a gap for a unit owner? What should you confirm?

**L.6.12 A:** An assessment may create an out-of-pocket obligation for the owner. Confirm why the assessment was made, what it funds, and whether the owner’s coverage addresses that type of obligation.

**L.6.13 Q:** Does an association deductible automatically become a covered loss assessment? Why not?

**L.6.14 A:** No, the deductible amount billed to an owner is not automatically covered. Coverage depends on the reason for the assessment, the applicable policy terms, and any relevant exclusions.

**L.6.15 Q:** Why should you separate physical damage from an assessment notice? Can they be handled the same way?

**L.6.16 A:** Physical damage concerns what property was harmed and who insures it. An assessment notice concerns an obligation imposed on an owner, so it requires its own coverage analysis.

**L.6.17 Q:** What should you do when the association says the damage is “inside the unit”? Is that enough information?

**L.6.18 A:** Treat that statement as a starting point, not a final coverage decision. Ask what was damaged, who maintained it, and how the applicable coverage assigns responsibility.

**L.6.19 Q:** Why does original construction matter in a master policy gap review? What can happen when it is unclear?

**L.6.20 A:** Original construction can help distinguish association property from owner-installed property. When it is unclear, the file may need stronger support before responsibility can be determined.

**L.6.21 Q:** Can a unit owner’s personal property be covered by the master policy? What is the usual concern?

**L.6.22 A:** Personal property is often treated differently from building property held by the association. Do not assume the master policy protects an owner’s belongings simply because the loss occurred in the unit.

**L.6.23 Q:** How can water damage expose a condominium coverage gap? What facts should you gather?

**L.6.24 A:** Water can damage shared building components, unit finishes, owner improvements, and personal property at the same time. Gather the source of water, the damaged property, and the party responsible for each item.

**L.6.25 Q:** Does the source of water matter when reviewing coverage? How should you use that fact?

**L.6.26 A:** Yes, the source may affect which coverage applies and whether an exclusion is relevant. Record the facts without assuming that every form of water damage has the same result.

**L.6.27 Q:** What is the underwriting value of understanding the association’s maintenance responsibility? How does it affect the gap analysis?

**L.6.28 A:** Maintenance responsibility can indicate which party is expected to repair a property component. It does not replace coverage review, but it can identify areas where disputes or uninsured costs may arise.

**L.6.29 Q:** Why should you ask whether damaged property served the whole building or only a unit? What might that reveal?

**L.6.30 A:** Property serving the whole building may be more likely to fall within association responsibility. Property serving only a unit may require closer review of owner responsibility and coverage wording.

**L.6.31 Q:** Can exterior property create a gap for an association or unit owner? What should you avoid assuming?

**L.6.32 A:** Exterior property can create uncertainty because ownership, maintenance, and insurance responsibility may not align. Avoid assuming that property outside the unit is always protected by the master policy.

**L.6.33 Q:** Why is the per-item landscaping limit relevant to an underwriter? What should you consider?

**L.6.34 A:** The per-item landscaping limit can restrict recovery for damaged exterior property. Consider how landscaping values, maintenance obligations, and loss exposure may affect the association.

**L.6.35 Q:** How can a roof loss affect unit owners even when the roof is association property? What should you investigate?

**L.6.36 A:** A roof loss can lead to assessments, interior damage, delayed repairs, or disputes over resulting damage. Investigate the damaged property, the source of loss, and the association’s planned response.

**L.6.37 Q:** Why might roof survey age matter during underwriting? What does it help you understand?

**L.6.38 A:** Roof survey age may affect confidence in the current condition information available for the risk. It can help you identify whether the file supports a clear view of roof-related exposure.

**L.6.39 Q:** What is the named storm period issue in a condominium claim? Why should adjusters pay attention?

**L.6.40 A:** Named storm provisions can affect deductibles, coverage triggers, and claim handling. You should verify the applicable event status rather than relying on informal descriptions of the weather.

**L.6.41 Q:** Why should you confirm the hours after a named storm designation ends that the named storm period lasts? What decision can it affect?

**L.6.42 A:** That information may affect whether named storm terms still apply to the loss. Match the reported timing to the applicable coverage terms and the verified event information.

**L.6.43 Q:** Can ordinance or law costs create a master policy gap? What type of expenses may be involved?

**L.6.44 A:** Ordinance or law costs can arise when repair work must meet current requirements. The association and unit owner may not have the same protection for those added costs.

**L.6.45 Q:** Why should you distinguish repair cost from upgrade cost? How does that help the claim review?

**L.6.46 A:** Repair cost restores damaged property, while upgrade cost may result from requirements or voluntary improvements. Separating them helps prevent unsupported assumptions about what coverage should pay.

**L.6.47 Q:** Can a vacancy issue affect a condominium loss? What information should you obtain?

**L.6.48 A:** Vacancy can affect risk conditions, maintenance, discovery of damage, and applicable coverage provisions. Obtain clear facts about occupancy, supervision, and when the damage was discovered.

**L.6.49 Q:** Why is prompt reporting important when an association loss may affect many owners? What can delay create?

**L.6.50 A:** Prompt reporting supports inspection, mitigation, and clearer communication about responsibility. Delay can increase damage and make it harder to determine the cause and scope of loss.

**L.6.51 Q:** What is the prompt payment period, and why should claim staff understand it? How should it be used?

**L.6.52 A:** The prompt payment period guides expectations for claim handling after required information is available. Use it with the applicable claim facts and avoid treating it as a substitute for a complete investigation.

**L.6.53 Q:** What is the safest final habit when reviewing a possible master policy gap? What should you document?

**L.6.54 A:** Identify the damaged property, the responsible party, the applicable coverage, and any unresolved conflict. Document the facts supporting your conclusion and clearly note issues that require further review.
